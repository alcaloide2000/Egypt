4TH DYNASTY · THE SUMMIT

Giza — Great Pyramid

Built by Khufu, Sneferu's son — the payoff of his father's trial and error at Meidum and Dahshur. The largest pyramid ever built and the last-standing Wonder of the Ancient World, it held the record for tallest human-made structure for roughly 3,800 years.

Khafre — Second Pyramid

Khufu's son, building right next to him. His pyramid is smaller than his father's, but sits on 10 m higher bedrock — the trick that makes it look taller on the skyline. It's also the only one of Giza's three still capped with its original polished limestone casing near the summit, a hint of how all of them once shone. The Great Sphinx, carved from the bedrock along his causeway, is generally attributed to him too.

Great Sphinx

A lion's body with a king's face, carved directly from a single outcrop of bedrock left over from quarrying stone for Khafre's pyramid complex — not built up from blocks like everything else on this map, but cut down into shape. It faces due east along Khafre's causeway, and is generally, though not universally, identified as his portrait.

Menkaure — Third Pyramid

The smallest and last of Giza's three, roughly a tenth the volume of his grandfather Khufu's — Old Kingdom pyramid-building was already running out of resources, or ambition, or both. Its lower courses are still clad in polished red granite hauled 900 km down the Nile from Aswan, casing that stops abruptly partway up, unfinished when Menkaure died. Three small queens' pyramids line up beside it.

NORTH · NEAR CAIRO

Saqqara — Step Pyramid

The necropolis of ancient Memphis, on the Nile's west bank. Djoser's Step Pyramid, designed by his architect Imhotep in the Third Dynasty, is the world's oldest large-scale cut-stone monument. It grew in stages rather than as one design: a square mastaba first, then enlarged outward and upward at least twice more, each new shell built around the last, before settling into its final six-tiered form.

Gisr el-Mudir — Great Enclosure

A vast enclosure of unworked stone, a few hundred metres west of the Step Pyramid — its rough dry-stone walls, 15 m thick at the base, predate Djoser's monument. No structure has ever been found inside, so its purpose is unknown, but its resemblance to Khasekhemwy's enclosures at Hierakonpolis and Abydos suggests the same king may have built all three.

Ti — Mastaba of Ti

The one tomb on this whole map that doesn't belong to a king. Ti was a high official — overseer of the sun temples and pyramids — who served several 5th-Dynasty pharaohs, and his mastaba near Djoser's enclosure holds the finest non-royal reliefs of the Old Kingdom: farming, boatbuilding, a hippopotamus hunt in the marshes. It's less a monument to power than a record of what everyday life looked like while the pyramids were going up.

FOUND HERE TOO · MUCH LATER

Serapeum — Apis Bull Catacombs

The odd one out in this cluster: everything else at Saqqara on this map is Old Kingdom, but the Serapeum is New Kingdom and later — underground galleries holding the mummified bodies of the sacred Apis bulls, worshipped as living images of Ptah. Individual burials began around 1390 BC; Khaemwaset, a son of Ramesses II, turned it into a single connected tunnel system in the 19th Dynasty, and it stayed in use into the Ptolemaic period — the same centuries as Philae, at the opposite end of this map.

FOUND HERE TOO · LAST OF THE 5TH

Unas — Pyramid Texts

The last king of the 5th Dynasty, with a small, unremarkable pyramid at Saqqara — except for what's carved inside it. Unas was the first to fill his burial chamber with hundreds of spells meant to protect and resurrect him: the Pyramid Texts, the oldest religious writing known anywhere. Every pyramid before his was silent inside; from here on, that changed, and these spells would echo down through the Coffin Texts to the Book of the Dead over a thousand years later.

Teti — First of the 6th

Unas died without the succession settled, and Teti — possibly through marriage into the old royal line — became the first king of the 6th Dynasty, near Djoser's pyramid. FOUND HERE TOO · A CITY'S NAME

Pepy — Pepi I & II

Pepi I named his South Saqqara pyramid complex Men-nefer-Pepi, "Pepi's perfection endures." That name outlived him: shortened to Mennefer, it eventually eclipsed the older name of the nearby capital entirely and passed into Greek as Memphis — the very city every card on this map keeps calling Saqqara's necropolis. His son Pepi II then reigned an almost unbelievable length, traditionally 94 years, taking the throne as a child and probably outliving his own heirs — a reign so long that Old Kingdom Egypt's administration was already unraveling by its end.

NO PIN · A NAME MISREAD

Netjerkare Siptah — "Nitocris"

The seventh and last ruler of the 6th Dynasty, reigning briefly as central authority collapsed into the First Intermediate Period — no pyramid, no fixed point on this map, just a name at the very end of the king list Unas and Pepi's line had kept for two centuries. For millennia, Herodotus and Manetho knew that name as Nitocris, a legendary queen who avenged her brother's murder by drowning the killers at a banquet. Egyptologist Kim Ryholt showed in 2000 that "Nitocris" was a scribal garbling of "Netjerkare" — she never existed; he did, and the story was never hers.

FOUND HERE TOO · THE LAST ONE

Ibi — Qakare Ibi

The last pyramid ever built at Saqqara, full stop — small, hastily made, northeast of Shepseskaf's sarcophagus tomb and by the causeway of Pepi II, whose 94-year reign had already outlasted the government's ability to build like this again. Ibi ruled barely two years from a Memphis that no longer controlled much of Egypt, one of fourteen shadowy 8th-Dynasty kings during the collapse into the First Intermediate Period. After him, no one at Saqqara raised a pyramid for over 300 years.

NO PIN · POSSIBLY LEGENDARY

Mentuhotep I — Tepy-a, "the Ancestor"

Listed in some king lists as the founder of the Theban 11th Dynasty, a nomarch and father of Intef the Elder — but his very own name, "Tepy-a," means "the ancestor," and was clearly given to him after death, not during a reign. No contemporary monument or tomb can be safely attributed to him, which has led many Egyptologists to suspect he's a retroactive invention: a tidy founding father, credited by the dynasty that rose under Intef II and Mentuhotep II, decades after whatever he actually was.

THEBES · FORERUNNER

Intef II — Wahankh

Third king of the Theban 11th Dynasty, ruling nearly fifty years while Egypt stayed split between his line and rival kings at Herakleopolis to the north. He pushed Theban control up the Nile as far as Abydos, laying the groundwork his successors would finish. He was buried at El-Tarif in a "saff-tomb" — Arabic for "row" — a long row of rock-cut pillars fronting a vast courtyard. A biographical stela from the tomb shows him seated above five hunting dogs, three still legible by name: Behekay, Abaqer, and Pehetez.

THEBES · REUNIFIER

Mentuhotep II — Deir el-Bahari

A century of chaos after Ibi's collapse ends here. Ruling from Thebes, Mentuhotep II fought his way north and eventually reunified Egypt, closing out the First Intermediate Period and founding the Middle Kingdom. He marked the moment with something no one had tried before: not a pyramid, but a temple cut into the cliffs of the Theban west bank, its colonnaded terraces climbing the rock face in steps. Five centuries later, Hatshepsut built her own temple right beside it, deliberately echoing his design.

12TH DYNASTY · FOUNDER

Amenemhat I — Sehetepibre

Mentuhotep II's Middle Kingdom didn't last as a single line — by the time Amenemhat I took the throne, likely a former vizier who may have seized power rather than inherited it, the 11th Dynasty had run out. He founded the 12th, and shifted the capital away from Thebes entirely, north to a new city called Itjtawy near the Faiyum. His pyramid at Lisht, close to the new capital, reused stone quarried straight from Giza and Saqqara's Old Kingdom monuments — some blocks still carry older kings' names, carved centuries before. He may have set up a coregency with his son Senusret I near the end of his reign, which didn't save him: the Story of Sinuhe and his own "Instructions" both describe him being assassinated by his own guards.

12TH DYNASTY · CONSOLIDATOR

Senusret I — Kheperkare

Amenemhat I's son and, for roughly a decade before his father's death, his coregent — the arrangement that let him move fast when it mattered. The Story of Sinuhe opens with him racing back from a military campaign in Libya the moment word reaches him that his father has been assassinated, to secure the throne before anyone else can. He held it for around 45 years, pushing Egypt's southern border deep into Nubia and building at temples across the country, most famously the White Chapel at Karnak — a small limestone kiosk covered in exceptionally fine relief, built for his jubilee festival. Later kings tore it down and used its blocks as filler inside a pylon; restorers found the pieces still in good order in 1927 and rebuilt it. His own pyramid stands beside his father's at Lisht.

TOD · TRADE NETWORK

Tod — Djerty, Temple of Montu

A small temple 20 km south of Luxor, dedicated to the falcon war-god Montu, easy to overlook next to Karnak and Luxor Temple — until 1936, when excavator Fernand Bisson de la Roque found four bronze chests buried in the temple floor, each stamped with Amenemhat II's name. Inside: over 150 silver cups and bowls, some in styles that trace back to the Aegean, and lapis lazuli by the thousand piece — raw fragments, beads, and cylinder seals carved in Mesopotamia. None of it was mined or made in Egypt. The Tod Treasure is some of the clearest physical proof of just how far Middle Kingdom trade networks actually reached.

12TH DYNASTY · WARRIOR KING

Senusret III — Khakaure

The most powerful king of the Middle Kingdom, and arguably the model for the legendary conquering "Sesostris" that Greek writers like Herodotus described centuries later. He drove at least four campaigns into Nubia, then built a chain of massive mudbrick fortresses — Semna, Kumma, Uronarti, Buhen — to hold the border at the Second Cataract, and issued a decree at Semna in his Year 8 fixing exactly who could cross it. At home he curbed the power of provincial nomarchs, pulling authority back to the crown. His statues break from centuries of idealized portraiture: heavy-lidded eyes, a furrowed brow, jug ears — an unmistakably careworn face, on purpose. His pyramid stands at Dahshur, dwarfed today by Sneferu's much older ones nearby.

FOUND HERE TOO · MOST FAMOUS PORTRAIT

Statue of Senusret III — found at Deir el-Bahari

The single most reproduced image of Senusret III: a life-size standing figure in dark, polished granodiorite, buried for safekeeping — or as an offering — inside Mentuhotep II's much older temple at Deir el-Bahari, on the very site now shared with his own hover marker above. It's one of a set of nearly identical statues found together, split apart by the archaeology market: this one usually cited as the type example is in the British Museum, with close relatives in the Metropolitan Museum of Art and the Egyptian Museum, Cairo. More than any building he commissioned, it's this face — heavy-lidded, deep-set eyes, a mouth set in something between resolve and exhaustion — that fixed how Senusret III is remembered.

17TH DYNASTY · SECOND INTERMEDIATE

Nubkheperre Intef — Antef VI

Three centuries after Senusret III, Egypt has split again — a Hyksos dynasty rules the north from Avaris while a rump Theban dynasty, the 17th, holds the south. Nubkheperre Intef was one of its kings, reigning only a handful of years, but he left an unusually clear paper trail for someone this obscure: a decree carved into the wall of the Min temple at Gebtu, sacking a corrupt temple official named Teti, and a small pyramid-topped tomb at Dra Abu el-Naga on the Theban west bank — one of the few Second Intermediate Period royal tombs ever positively identified. His gilded coffin was pulled out by tomb robbers in 1827 and bought by the British Museum in 1835, decades before archaeologists caught up with where it had come from.

17TH DYNASTY · LAST OF THE LINE

Kamose — Wadjkheperre

A few kings after Nubkheperre Intef, and the war with the Hyksos has turned from defensive to personal. His predecessor (probably his brother) Seqenenre Tao likely died fighting them — his mummy shows brutal, unhealed axe wounds to the skull. Kamose picked up where he left off: in his short reign he sailed a fleet north into Hyksos territory, boasting about it in two vivid, first-person stelae set up at Karnak (later found reused as filler inside the temple's own pylons, the same fate that befell Senusret I's White Chapel). One stela describes intercepting a letter from the Hyksos king Apophis, begging the ruler of Kush for help against Thebes — proof, in Kamose's telling, that the enemy was afraid. He died without finishing the war; his brother Ahmose I would go on to expel the Hyksos entirely and found the New Kingdom.

18TH DYNASTY · FOUNDER

Ahmose I — Nebpehtyre

Kamose's brother finished what he started. Ahmose I took Avaris, the Hyksos capital in the Delta, then pursued them out of Egypt entirely and laid siege to their stronghold at Sharuhen in Canaan — Egypt reunified under native rule for the first time in over a century, and by convention, the New Kingdom begins here. He also pushed back into Nubia to restore the southern border his 17th-Dynasty predecessors had lost. At South Abydos he raised a pyramid, paired with its own terraced temple — not a tomb this time but a cenotaph, a symbolic monument. It was buried and forgotten until archaeologists identified it in 1899–1902, and it has never been surpassed: no native Egyptian king ever built another pyramid after this one.

Ahmose's Temple — terrace temple, South Abydos

Beside the pyramid, cut into the low desert terrace, stood a temple whose walls were once covered in painted relief telling the story of the war itself — chariot horses, archers massed three deep, a royal battle fleet with oars in the water, and fallen Hyksos soldiers identifiable by their fringed, non-Egyptian dress. 18TH DYNASTY · DEIFIED QUEEN

Ahmose-Nefertari — Lady of the West

Ahmose I's sister-wife, and mother of his successor Amenhotep I, carried the title "God's Wife of Amun" — a religious office that would grow into real political power over the following centuries. But it's what happened after she died that made her extraordinary: she and her son were deified and worshipped for roughly 400 years as the patron gods of the Theban west bank, credited with founding the workmen's village at Deir el-Medina and honored in chapel after chapel there long after any living memory of them remained. She's almost always shown with black skin in this deified form — not a record of complexion, but a color tied to the fertile Nile silt and to Osiris, god of the afterlife and rebirth. Her mummy, like so many New Kingdom royals, was moved for safekeeping in antiquity and rediscovered in the Deir el-Bahari cache in 1881.

18TH DYNASTY · UNTOUCHED MUMMY

Amenhotep I — Djeserkare

Ahmose I and Ahmose-Nefertari's son ruled about 21 years, campaigning into Nubia to hold what his father had won back, and founding Set-Ma'at — the workmen's village later called Deir el-Medina — where he and his mother would be worshipped as patron gods for centuries. He's credited with a genuine innovation in royal burial: the first king known to build his mortuary temple somewhere else entirely from his actual tomb, precisely to make the tomb harder to find and rob. It worked almost too well — his tomb has never been located. His mummy turned up in the Deir el-Bahari cache in 1881, rewrapped twice by 21st-Dynasty priests, and it remains the only royal mummy modern archaeologists have chosen never to unwrap, out of respect for its pristine floral collar — until a 2021 CT scan finally let researchers see beneath the linen without disturbing a single layer.

THEBAN NECROPOLIS · OFFICIALS, NOT KINGS

Tombs of the Nobles — Sheikh Abd el-Qurna

While kings hid their burials in the Valley of the Kings, the officials who served them were buried in plain sight on the hillside of Sheikh Abd el-Qurna, in small painted tomb-chapels open to visiting relatives. Freed from royal funerary conventions, these walls show what royal tombs never bothered to: banquets with musicians and dancers, farming and fowling scenes, workshops, and tribute from foreign lands — the liveliest record of daily life to survive from ancient Egypt. The most famous belongs to Rekhmire (TT100), vizier under Thutmose III and Amenhotep II, whose tomb preserves both dazzling scenes of foreign tribute-bearers and a long inscribed text known as the "Duties of the Vizier," the fullest surviving description of how Egypt's government actually worked. Nearby, Nakht's tomb (Theban Tomb 52) and Menna's (TT69) are prized for their especially vivid banquet and Nile-marsh scenes. Because the necropolis holds hundreds of officials' tombs spanning many reigns and dynasties rather than one dateable monument, it isn't marked on the timeline below.

THEBES · TWO THOUSAND YEARS BUILDING

Karnak — Ipet-sut

Ipet-sut means "the most select of places" — a fitting name for the largest religious complex ever built. Karnak isn't one temple but dozens, layered over roughly two millennia: Senusret I raised its earliest known shrine here (his White Chapel, later dismantled and rediscovered as pylon filler), a chain of kings expanded it dynasty after dynasty, and its centerpiece, the Precinct of Amun-Re, still holds the Great Hypostyle Hall — 134 giant papyrus columns across 5,000 m², the tallest 21 metres high, begun by Seti I and finished by his son Ramesses II. Kamose's own victory stelae, celebrating his war against the Hyksos, were later found reused as filler inside one of Karnak's pylons — the same fate that befell Senusret I's chapel, seven centuries earlier. Nowhere else on this map does one site carry so much of Egypt's history at once.

18TH DYNASTY · EMPIRE BUILDER

Thutmose I — Aakheperkare

Amenhotep I died without a surviving son, and the throne passed to Thutmose I — likely a military commander with no direct royal blood, married into legitimacy through a queen named Ahmose. Whatever his origins, he ruled like a conqueror: he pushed south past the Nile's fourth cataract deeper into Nubia than any king before him, then marched all the way to the Euphrates in Syria, where his soldiers marveled at a river that appeared to "flow backward" — south to north was normal to them, and the Euphrates runs the other way. At Karnak he added pylons and raised a pair of obelisks before the temple; one still stands where he set it. And following Amenhotep I's precedent, he had his architect Ineni cut his tomb into a hidden valley on the Theban west bank — the first king confirmed buried in what would become the Valley of the Kings.

18TH DYNASTY · RECENT DISCOVERY

Thutmose II — Aakheperenre

A lesser son of Thutmose I, married to his own fully royal half-sister Hatshepsut to secure his claim — and by most accounts dominated by her throughout his reign. When Nubia rebelled at his coronation, and Bedouin raiders stirred trouble in Sinai, it was his generals who put both down, not the king himself. For a figure this overshadowed, his afterlife has been unexpectedly dramatic: his tomb, tucked in the Western Wadis near the Valley of the Queens, was found in 2022 and only confirmed as his in 2025 — the first new royal tomb identified since Tutankhamun's in 1922. It had flooded not long after his burial; the ceiling's painted blue sky and yellow stars survive only in damaged fragments, and his body was later moved for safekeeping to the Deir el-Bahari cache, found in 1881.

18TH DYNASTY · FEMALE PHARAOH

Hatshepsut — Maatkare

Thutmose II's widow, ruling first as regent for his young son Thutmose III — then, within a few years, as king in her own right, one of very few women in Egyptian history to hold the full title of pharaoh. Statues and reliefs increasingly show her with a male king's regalia and a ceremonial false beard, less a disguise than a costume the role itself demanded. Her defining project was Djeser-djeseru, "Holy of Holies," a vast terraced temple at Deir el-Bahari built right beside Mentuhotep II's five centuries older one and deliberately echoing it — designed by her steward Senenmut, its walls record a trading expedition to the land of Punt in extraordinary detail, down to potted incense trees carried home live on the ships. After her death, Thutmose III had many of her images chiseled out and her cartouches walled over at Karnak — a systematic erasure that took Egyptologists generations to fully recognize for what it was.

18TH DYNASTY · THE NAPOLEON OF EGYPT

Thutmose III — Menkheperre

Hatshepsut's stepson and co-ruler spent over twenty years in her shadow, then, after her death, ruled alone for three more decades and became the most successful military king Egypt ever produced — sometimes called its Napoleon. In Year 23 he led his army through the narrow Aruna pass to surprise a rebel coalition at Megiddo, in modern Israel, a gamble his own officers warned against; it worked, and the ensuing siege became the first battle in history recorded in enough tactical detail to reconstruct. That campaign and roughly sixteen others are carved onto the walls at Karnak as the Annals of Thutmose III, a year-by-year military ledger in stone. He was buried in the Valley of the Kings at KV34, the first tomb where the complete Book of Amduat — the sun god's night journey through the underworld — was found painted on the walls. He also had many of Hatshepsut's monuments defaced late in his reign, though historians still debate how much of that was personal animus versus routine dynastic politics.

18TH DYNASTY · GOLDEN AGE

Amenhotep III — Nebmaatre

Two generations after Thutmose III's conquests, Amenhotep III inherited the wealthiest, most secure Egypt had ever been — and spent his 38-year reign building on a scale to match, more than almost any king before him. On the Theban west bank he raised a mortuary temple covering some 35 hectares, the largest ever attempted in Egypt; nearly all of it later collapsed and was quarried away, leaving only its two seated guardian statues, the Colossi of Memnon, still standing in the sand. Nearby he built Malkata, a sprawling mudbrick palace city with its own harbor lake, dug for his jubilee festivals and grand enough to have functioned as a second capital. At Luxor Temple he began the great papyrus-column colonnade leading to its entrance — left unfinished at his death and completed decades later by Tutankhamun and Horemheb. His Great Royal Wife, Tiye, held unusually visible power and appears alongside him on statues and scarabs at genuinely equal scale, and their son would become the revolutionary Akhenaten.

18TH DYNASTY · THE HERETIC

Akhenaten — Amenhotep IV, Neferkheperure

Amenhotep III's son began his reign as Amenhotep IV, but by Year 5 he had remade Egypt's religion around a single god: the Aten, the sun disc itself, worshipped without the usual crowd of animal-headed deities or their priesthoods. He renamed himself Akhenaten, "Effective for the Aten," closed the temples of Amun and the other gods, and abandoned Thebes entirely for a brand-new capital built from nothing on virgin desert: Akhetaten, "Horizon of the Aten," marked out by boundary stelae cut into the cliffs on both sides of the Nile. Amarna art broke just as sharply from tradition — elongated faces and bodies, and intimate scenes of the royal family lounging together under the Aten's rays, each ray ending in a tiny hand, some offering the ankh directly to the king and his queen, Nefertiti, with no priest standing between them. The experiment didn't survive him: his successors, ending with Tutankhamun, abandoned Amarna, restored the old gods, and later king lists skip Akhenaten entirely, folding his reign into others' — Egypt's first attempt at erasing a pharaoh from history.

18TH DYNASTY · GREAT ROYAL WIFE

Nefertiti — Neferneferuaten Nefertiti

Akhenaten's Great Royal Wife appears everywhere in Amarna art with a prominence royal wives rarely had before: worshipping the Aten at his side as an equal, riding in her own chariot, even smiting enemies in the traditional pose reserved for kings. Some Egyptologists think she ruled briefly in her own right after his death, under the throne name Neferneferuaten, before Tutankhamun's reign — a theory still debated, since the evidence is fragmentary and her fate after Year 12 of Akhenaten's reign simply goes dark; she vanishes from the record with no tomb or mummy ever confirmed. What survives instead is her face: a painted limestone bust found in 1912 in the workshop of the sculptor Thutmose, right here at Amarna, made as a master sculptor's model rather than a finished royal commission — which is likely why her left eye's inlay was never fitted. Now in Berlin's Neues Museum, it's become the single most recognizable image of an ancient Egyptian.

18TH DYNASTY · THE INTACT TOMB

Tutankhamun — Nebkheperure

Born Tutankhaten, likely the son of Akhenaten, he came to the throne around age nine or ten in the chaos left by the Amarna experiment, and — guided by older advisors, chief among them Ay and the general Horemheb — reversed it: the court moved back to Thebes, the old gods and their temples were restored, and the boy king changed his own name to Tutankhamun to match. He died around age eighteen or nineteen, cause still debated (a broken leg, malaria, and health problems likely rooted in his parents being siblings are the leading theories), leaving a tomb built in haste and small by royal standards. That smallness may have saved it: robbers broke in twice within decades of the burial but never finished the job, and the entrance was eventually buried under debris from later tomb-building and forgotten. Howard Carter, funded by Lord Carnarvon, found it sealed in 1922 after years of searching — over 5,000 objects packed into four small rooms, including the solid gold mask still on his mummy, one of the only Valley of the Kings tombs found with its royal burial essentially intact.

18TH DYNASTY · THE GENERAL-KING

Horemheb — Djeserkheperure

A career army commander who served as Tutankhamun's general and effective regent, Horemheb took the throne himself after the brief reign of the elderly courtier Ay, becoming the last king of the 18th Dynasty. He pushed the restoration of the old gods further than either of his predecessors: his Restoration Edict at Karnak describes temples in ruins and priesthoods scattered, and he set about rebuilding both. He also began quietly erasing the entire Amarna episode from official memory, folding Akhenaten's, Tutankhamun's, and Ay's regnal years into his own count — later king lists skip straight from Amenhotep III to Horemheb. At Karnak he raised new pylons, and had them packed inside with thousands of small inscribed talatat blocks torn from Akhenaten's dismantled Aten temples — recycling the heretic's own monuments as rubble fill for the restoration. Having no surviving heir, he chose his vizier and fellow military man Paramessu as successor, who took the throne as Ramesses I and founded the 19th Dynasty.

19TH DYNASTY · FOUNDER

Ramesses I — Menpehtyre

Born Paramessu, a career soldier and vizier from a military family in the northeastern Delta, he was already old when the childless Horemheb named him heir — a safe, experienced hand to found a new line. He reigned barely a year or two, too briefly to build much beyond a small tomb and some early work at Karnak later expanded by his son and grandson, but it was enough: he founded the 19th Dynasty, whose next two kings, Seti I and Ramesses II, would preside over one of Egypt's last great imperial peaks. His own afterlife took a stranger path than either of theirs. His mummy vanished from history and turned up, unidentified, in a Niagara Falls curiosity museum sometime in the 19th century, displayed for decades among oddities with no one aware of who lay inside. When the museum closed in 1999, Atlanta's Michael C. Carlos Museum acquired the collection; CT scans, embalming style, and a striking resemblance to his son and grandson's mummies confirmed his identity, and in 2003 Ramesses I was flown home to Egypt with full honors, more than two centuries after he'd disappeared from it.

19TH DYNASTY · THE RESTORER

Seti I — Menmaatre

Ramesses I's son inherited a throne barely a year old and set about proving the new dynasty could match the old ones for ambition. He campaigned hard in his first years, striking at Shasu Bedouin in Sinai, Hittite-allied cities in Syria, and Libyan raiders in the west, and had the victories carved in raised relief across Karnak's northern exterior wall — some of the best-preserved battle scenes to survive from Egypt. Inside, he continued the Great Hypostyle Hall, its 134 columns eventually finished by his son Ramesses II. At Abydos he built a temple unlike any other, its seven parallel sanctuaries carrying the Abydos King List, 76 royal cartouches tracing an unbroken line of kings back to Menes. But his masterpiece is his own tomb: over 100 metres long and 30 metres deep, the first in the Valley of the Kings decorated from entrance to burial chamber without a break, its ceiling painted with the earliest known complete map of the Egyptian night sky. Discovered by Giovanni Belzoni in 1817, it remains one of the most spectacular tombs ever found there. His mummy, recovered from the Deir el-Bahari cache in 1881, is among the best preserved of any pharaoh — his face still startlingly recognizable after three thousand years.

19TH DYNASTY · THE GREAT

Ramesses II — Usermaatre Setepenre

Seti I's son ruled for 66 years, longer than almost any king before him, and spent them building on a scale meant to outlast every rival's memory — Abu Simbel, the completed Karnak Hypostyle Hall, a new Delta capital called Pi-Ramesses, and his own mortuary temple, the Ramesseum, fronted by a seated colossus so immense its shattered fragments still stop visitors cold three thousand years later; it's the likely inspiration behind Shelley's sonnet "Ozymandias." In Year 5 he led his army against the Hittite Empire at Kadesh in Syria — nearly ambushed and saved only by reinforcements, he claimed total victory anyway on temple walls across Egypt. Neither side had actually won, and sixteen years later the two empires signed the Treaty of Kadesh, the oldest surviving peace treaty in the world, inscribed in Karnak's own stone. He married more wives and fathered more children (over 100) than any pharaoh on record; his favorite, Nefertari, got a tomb in the Valley of the Queens considered the most beautifully painted in Egypt. His mummy, recovered in 1881, was flown to Paris in 1976 for fungus treatment — and, since French law required travel papers for human remains crossing the border, Egypt issued him a passport listing his occupation as "King (deceased)."

19TH DYNASTY · THE ISRAEL STELE

Merneptah — Baenre

Ramesses II outlived his first twelve sons, so the throne passed to his thirteenth, already an old man himself, probably in his sixties. He proved it hadn't gone to someone past his prime: in Year 5 a coalition of Libyan raiders and roving "Sea Peoples" — displaced groups from the Aegean and Asia Minor, first clearly recorded here — marched on the Nile Delta, and Merneptah's army crushed them, killing or capturing thousands. He had the victory carved onto a granite stela originally raised by Amenhotep III, reused at his own mortuary temple in Thebes. Its closing lines, almost an afterthought after the main Libyan campaign, boast of subduing Canaan and list defeated peoples and cities — Ashkelon, Gezer, Yenoam, and "Israel," written with a marker for a people rather than a place. It's the oldest surviving mention of Israel from any source, Egyptian or otherwise, and the single line "Israel is laid waste, his seed is not" has made this modest victory stela one of the most argued-over inscriptions in Egyptology. He was buried in the Valley of the Kings; his mummy turned up in 1898, stashed in Amenhotep II's tomb along with a cache of other displaced royals.

19TH DYNASTY · CONTESTED THRONE

Seti II — Userkheperure Setepenre

Merneptah's son inherited a throne that didn't stay settled for long: within a couple of years a rival, Amenmesse — possibly a half-brother — seized Thebes and the south, ruling it as a separate kingdom for several years while Seti II held on elsewhere. When Seti II finally regained full control, his own tomb, KV15, had to be repaired: Amenmesse's supporters, or Amenmesse himself, had defaced its royal names during the split, and Seti had them recut. Amid the turmoil he still found time to build: a small, elegant triple chapel before Karnak's Second Pylon, a way station where the sacred barques of Amun, Mut, and Khonsu could rest during festival processions between temples. His chief wife, Twosret, would outlive him, act as regent for his young successor Siptah, and eventually claim the throne herself — one of the few women in Egyptian history to rule as pharaoh in her own right. Seti II's mummy was found in 1898, stashed with his father Merneptah's in the tomb of Amenhotep II, part of the same cache of relocated royals.

19TH DYNASTY · CHILD KING

Siptah — Akhenre Setepenre

A child when he came to the throne — parentage still debated, possibly a son of Seti II or of the rival king Amenmesse — Siptah never ruled in any real sense. Power sat with his stepmother Twosret, Seti II's widow, and with Bay, a chancellor of unusual and unexplained influence for someone born outside the royal family, who styled himself in inscriptions with a status close to the king's own. Both temple reliefs and Siptah's own mummy agree on one striking detail: his left leg was shortened and turned inward in a club foot, most likely the result of childhood polio, and he's shown in art leaning on a cane. The uneasy arrangement didn't last — an inscription from Elephantine records Bay's execution for an unnamed "great crime" in Year 5, and Siptah himself died not long after, still young, leaving Twosret to simply continue his regnal count and rule Egypt alone. His tomb, KV47, was built alongside Twosret's and Bay's in a matching architectural style, but it stood empty of his mummy for centuries until it turned up in 1898, cached with a group of other displaced New Kingdom royals.

19TH DYNASTY · FEMALE PHARAOH

Twosret — Sitre Meryamun

Seti II's Great Royal Wife spent years as the power behind two thrones before finally claiming one for herself. She acted as regent for the child king Siptah alongside the chancellor Bay, and when Siptah died young, she simply carried on — continuing his regnal count rather than starting her own, then dropping the pretense of regency altogether and ruling as pharaoh in full royal titulary, one of perhaps five or six women in three thousand years of Egyptian history to do so. She shared her tomb, KV14, with Seti II, and began a mortuary temple at Thebes near the Ramesseum. None of it survived her successors' opinion of her: her reign ended in what an inscription of the next king, Setnakhte, describes as a land in chaos, and Setnakhte's dynasty usurped her tomb, replastering and overwriting her image with his own, and tore down her mortuary temple so thoroughly that only its foundations remain. Her actual death and the transition to the 20th Dynasty are still poorly understood — one of the murkiest handovers in New Kingdom history.

20TH DYNASTY · FOUNDER

Setnakhte — Userkhaure

Setnakhte wasn't son, brother, or clear descendant of any recent king — probably a distant relative of the Ramesside family who seized the throne out of the confusion that closed the 19th Dynasty. His own account of it survives on a stela at Elephantine: Egypt in disorder, a usurper in power (later Egyptian tradition would blame "a Syrian," likely a swipe at the chancellor Bay or the general instability of Twosret's final years), and Setnakhte restoring order, reportedly paying off troublemakers in silver to leave peacefully rather than fighting them all. He began his own tomb, KV11, but abandoned it after his workmen accidentally broke into the older tomb of Amenmesse next door — a construction accident so awkward the site was simply given up. He was buried instead in Twosret's tomb, KV14, its walls replastered and her image overwritten with his own. His reign was short, but it steadied the throne enough to hand his son, Ramesses III, a stable Egypt — one that would need every bit of that stability for the invasions and crises still to come.

20TH DYNASTY · LAST GREAT WARRIOR

Ramesses III — Usermaatre Meryamun

Setnakhte's son ruled 31 years and is generally considered the last truly powerful pharaoh of the New Kingdom. In Year 8, a coalition of displaced raiders and migrants from the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean known as the Sea Peoples — the same pressure that had already toppled the Hittite Empire — invaded Egypt by land and sea. Ramesses III met and crushed them at the Battle of the Delta, and had the entire campaign carved in enormous relief across the walls of Medinet Habu, his fortified mortuary temple modeled with a Syrian-style migdol gate, as if the building itself were braced for war. The victory came at a cost: constant campaigning drained the treasury, grain deliveries to the royal tomb-workers' village fell behind, and in Year 29 the workmen at Deir el-Medina walked off the job — the earliest labor strike documented anywhere in history. He didn't live to see his reign's official end: a plot inside his own harem, led by a secondary wife named Tiye on behalf of her son Pentawere, killed him — confirmed in 2012 when a CT scan of his mummy revealed his throat had been cut. His legitimate heir, Ramesses IV, had the conspirators tried and executed.

20TH DYNASTY · THE CONSPIRATORS' TRIAL

Ramesses IV — Heqamaatre Setepenamun

Already middle-aged when his father was murdered, Ramesses IV opened his reign by putting the harem conspirators on trial and having roughly thirty of them executed. He then launched Egypt's largest stone-quarrying expedition on record, sending some 8,000 men into the Wadi Hammamat for building stone — a scale of ambition his six-year reign didn't leave time to finish. He's also indirectly responsible for one of Egyptology's richest sources: the Great Harris Papyrus, the longest known papyrus from ancient Egypt, was compiled listing his father's donations to the temples and completed early in his own reign, becoming a major record of late Ramesside religious and economic life. He was buried in KV2, a compact tomb with vividly painted astronomical ceilings.

20TH DYNASTY · A POSSIBLE EPIDEMIC

Ramesses V — Usermaatre Sekheperenre

A short, four-year reign left behind two remarkable documents. The Wilbour Papyrus, drawn up under him, is the most detailed land and tax survey to survive from ancient Egypt, recording plot by plot who farmed what land along a long stretch of the Nile and what they owed for it. His mummy tells a stranger story: raised, fluid-filled lesions cover his face, neck, and chest, a pattern many researchers have identified as one of the earliest suspected cases of smallpox in the historical record — though a burial delayed until his successor's second year, well past the customary 70 days, suggests his death and funeral were unusually troubled, possibly by exactly this kind of contagion.

20TH DYNASTY · TUTANKHAMUN'S ACCIDENTAL GUARDIAN

Ramesses VI — Nebmaatre Meryamun

Ramesses V's uncle took the throne after his young nephew's short reign and promptly took over his tomb too, usurping KV9 and recarving its unfinished sections with his own name. The workmen's huts and debris piled up outside KV9's entrance during its construction did Egyptology an enormous accidental favor: they buried the entrance to a much smaller tomb just below it — Tutankhamun's — under enough rubble to keep it hidden from the mass tomb-robbing of the following centuries. Without Ramesses VI's construction spoil, KV62 might well have been emptied like almost everything else in the valley long before Howard Carter arrived in 1922.

20TH DYNASTY · THE BRIEFEST REIGN

Ramesses VIII — Usermaatre Akhenamun

Egypt's least-documented Ramesside: a reign so short, perhaps as little as a single year, that almost nothing was built or recorded under his name, and no tomb has ever been securely identified as his. 20TH DYNASTY · THE TOMB ROBBERY TRIALS

Ramesses IX — Neferkare Setepenre

An eighteen-year reign, long by the standards of his immediate predecessors, remembered mainly for what it reveals about a system breaking down. The Tomb Robbery Papyri, court records from his reign, document officials investigating and prosecuting gangs who had been looting royal and private tombs across the Theban necropolis — testimony describing exactly how bodies were stripped of gold and coffins broken apart, and naming officials who took bribes to look away. His own tomb, KV6, sits directly opposite Tutankhamun's and has never been fully sealed off from visitors since antiquity, its walls layered with ancient graffiti left by tourists from the Greek and Roman periods.

20TH DYNASTY · LAST OF THE NEW KINGDOM

Ramesses XI — Menmaatre Setepenptah

The last king of the New Kingdom presided over its quiet unraveling rather than any single dramatic collapse. Real power in the south increasingly belonged to Herihor, the High Priest of Amun at Karnak, who eventually styled his own name in a royal cartouche — a de facto second kingship operating alongside the pharaoh's. The era's flavor survives in the Story of Wenamun, a literary account of an official sent to Byblos on the Lebanese coast to buy cedar for the sacred barge of Amun, who arrives to find Egyptian prestige abroad has collapsed so far that the local prince questions why he should hand anything over on credit at all. Ramesses XI began a tomb, KV4, but it was left unfinished and later stripped and reused as a workshop for restoring older royal mummies. When he died, Egypt split rather than passed cleanly to an heir: Smendes took the north from Tanis, Herihor's successors held Thebes as high priests, and the New Kingdom gave way to the fragmented Third Intermediate Period.

UPSTREAM · SAME AGE AS NARMER

Gebtu — Koptos, Temple of Min

North of Luxor at the desert's edge, Petrie excavated a temple of Min in 1894 and found, beneath its later Ptolemaic remains, an even older shrine of mud brick and timber — and with it, three colossal limestone statues of Min, roughly 4 metres tall, among the earliest large-scale sculpture known from Egypt. Gebtu also sat at the mouth of the Wadi Hammamat, the desert road to the Red Sea, making it a gateway for stone, gold, and trade throughout Egyptian history — later kings like Pepi I and Pepi II issued royal decrees here, carved straight into the temple's walls, and centuries after them the Theban king Nubkheperre Intef used the same wall to fire a corrupt temple official.

BEYOND EGYPT · KINGDOM OF KUSH

Meroë — Pyramids of Kush

Far south of Abu Simbel, in what is now Sudan, the Kushite kings built their own royal necropolis at Meroë — over 200 steep-sided pyramids, smaller than Egypt's but more than twice as numerous. According to the Greek historian Diodorus Siculus, King Arkamani broke the power of Amun's priests, who had traditionally been able to order a king to end his own life, and became the first king buried here instead of at the older capital, Napata, around 270 BC. Burials continued for some six centuries, into the 4th century AD. In the 1830s the Italian treasure hunter Giuseppe Ferlini decapitated over 40 of the pyramids searching for gold, finding a spectacular hoard of jewelry sealed in Queen Amanishakheto's — now split between museums in Berlin and Munich.
