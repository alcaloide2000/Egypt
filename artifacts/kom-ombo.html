Kom Ombo, the double temple

On a hill above a bend of the Nile, about 45 km north of Aswan, the Ptolemies built a temple for two gods at once. It is split down the middle: the northern half belongs to Haroeris, Horus the Elder, the falcon; the southern half to Sobek, the crocodile. Every gateway, hall and sanctuary is doubled, so there are two doors in every wall and two parallel paths to two altars.

Walking the temple, from the river to the gods

The visit comes up from the river through the gate, then heads east through the forecourt and the halls to the twin sanctuaries, round the corridors and out past the nilometer to the museum. Tap a number on the plan.

Simplified plan with approximate positions. North is up; the blue and green dashed lines are the two axes, for Haroeris and Sobek.

  1. 1

    The gate of Ptolemy XII

    The path from the river climbs to a gateway in the south side of the old mud-brick enclosure, put up by Ptolemy XII, Cleopatra's father, who also decorated the front halls. The hill, the kom, kept the temple above the flood.

    Ptolemy XII ↗

  2. 2

    The chapel of Hathor

    A small chapel near the gate, built under the Roman emperor Domitian. Mummified crocodiles from the local cemetery used to be piled up inside; they are now in the museum (12).

    Hathor ↗

  3. 3

    The birth house

    The mammisi, where the birth of the god's child was celebrated, stood west of the forecourt, but the river has carried most of it away. One surviving relief shows Ptolemy VIII in a papyrus boat in the marshes.

    Ptolemy VIII ↗

  4. 4

    The forecourt

    Decorated under the emperor Tiberius. Only the lower parts of its sixteen columns survive, some still painted. In the middle stands the base of an altar: from here on, everything comes in pairs.

  5. 5

    The outer hypostyle hall

    The façade has two doorways, one for each god. Inside, ten columns with flower capitals hold a ceiling still blue in places, with flying vultures. On the walls Ptolemy XII is purified and crowned by the gods, Horus and Thoth among them.

  6. 6

    The inner hypostyle hall

    A smaller, darker hall of ten columns, from Ptolemy VIII's time. On the walls the king offers to Sobek on one side and to Haroeris on the other.

  7. 7

    Three vestibules

    Three narrow rooms, each a step higher than the last, each with a door for each god: the floor rises and the light fades as you near the gods, as in every Egyptian temple.

  8. 8

    The twin sanctuaries

    Haroeris's sanctuary lies to the north and Sobek's to the south. Only the black granite bases of their shrines are left. Between the two, below the floor, runs a hidden passage; guides tell how a priest hid there to speak as the god's oracle.

    Sobek ↗ · Horus ↗

  9. 9

    The inner corridor

    A passage runs right round the sanctuaries, with small rooms opening off it. In its floor and walls are the openings of the crypts where the temple kept its treasures.

  10. 10

    The 'medical instruments'

    On the back wall of the outer corridor, a Roman-period relief shows a king offering a set of tools: knives, hooks, forceps, sponges and scales, beside two women on birthing chairs. It is often called a set of surgical instruments; some scholars think they are ritual tools.

  11. 11

    The nilometer

    A round well with a staircase winding down to the water table, north-west of the forecourt. Priests read the height of the flood on its walls, which set the year's taxes.

    Hapi ↗

  12. 12

    The Crocodile Museum

    Opened in 2012 just outside the enclosure: mummified crocodiles of every size, from hatchlings to adults, with their eggs and clay coffins, from the animal cemetery of Sobek nearby.

Kom Ombo through time

  1. before 200 BC

    A town called Nubt, 'the golden', Ombos in Greek, stands on the hill; blocks from an older temple were found on the site.

  2. c. 180–145 BC

    Ptolemy VI founds the present double temple.

  3. c. 145–116 BC

    Ptolemy VIII builds and decorates the inner halls, the vestibules and the sanctuaries.

  4. c. 80–51 BC

    Ptolemy XII adds the outer hypostyle hall and the gate.

  5. AD 14–37

    Under the emperor Tiberius the forecourt is decorated.

  6. AD 1st–2nd c.

    Roman emperors add the chapel of Hathor and carve the outer corridor, with the 'medical instruments'.

  7. later centuries

    Floods eat away the front of the site, earthquakes bring down the roofs and the stone is reused; sand fills the halls.

  8. 1893

    Jacques de Morgan clears the sand and restores the temple.

  9. 2012

    The Crocodile Museum opens beside the temple.

For our visit

Count the doors

Stand in the forecourt and look east: two doorways in every wall, one line for the falcon and one for the crocodile.

Look up

In the outer hypostyle hall the ceiling and capitals still keep their blue, green and red.

Go round the back

The 'medical instruments' are easy to miss: walk the outer corridor to the back wall of the temple.

Finish with the crocodiles

Leave time for the Crocodile Museum by the exit; it is small, cool and quick to see.
