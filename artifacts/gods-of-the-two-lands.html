Gods of the Two Lands

Egypt had hundreds of gods. Most began as the god of one town, and they rose or merged as their towns gained power. These are the ones you will meet most often on temple walls and in tombs, with how to recognise them and where to see them on the trip.

Four ideas that explain them

  • Animal heads are signs, not portraits. A god with a falcon or jackal head shows one of their powers. The same god can appear as an animal, a human or a mix.
  • Gods merge. Amun of Thebes joined the sun god to become Amun-Ra. Names were combined when two gods' roles met.
  • Families of three. Many temples housed a triad of father, mother and son, such as Amun, Mut and Khonsu at Karnak.
  • The king stands between gods and people. Only he, or priests acting for him, served the gods in the temple, feeding them, clothing them and keeping Ma'at, the order of the world.

Amun and Aten: not the same god

The names sound alike in English only by chance. In Egyptian they are Imen, "the hidden one", and Iten, "the disk", and they stand for opposite ideas: an invisible power behind everything, against the sun you can see.

How Aten replaced Amun. Akhenaten came to the throne as Amenhotep IV, a name that honours Amun. Within five years he had changed it, built Aten temples at Karnak itself, then left Thebes for a new capital in the desert at Amarna. One reason may have been to break the power of Amun's priests, who owned huge estates and could rival the king.

How Amun came back. After Akhenaten's death the boy king Tutankhaten became Tutankhamun, the court returned to Thebes and Amun's temples reopened. Horemheb later pulled down the Aten temples at Karnak and used their small, standard-sized blocks, the talatat, as filling inside his own pylons. Archaeologists have recovered tens of thousands of them.

Sun and creation

Ra

Re · Ra-Horakhty, Khepri, Atum (his forms through the day)

The sun, king of the gods

Look for
A man with a falcon head crowned by the sun disk, with a cobra (uraeus) coiled around it.
Home
Heliopolis (Iunu), now north-east Cairo.

Every day Ra crosses the sky in his boat. Every night he sails through the Duat, the underworld, and fights the serpent Apophis, the force of chaos, so that the sun can rise again. He was Khepri the scarab at dawn, Ra-Horakhty at midday and old Atum at dusk. From Dynasty 4 kings called themselves "Son of Ra", and the pyramids and obelisks were shaped after the sun's rays.

Amun

Amon, Amun-Ra · with his wife Mut and son Khonsu

"The hidden one", king of the gods in the New Kingdom

Look for
A man with a tall crown of two plumes, sometimes with blue skin; also shown as a ram.
Home
Thebes (Luxor): Karnak and Luxor Temple.

A local god of Thebes who rose with the Theban kings. When they reunited Egypt he was joined to the sun god as Amun-Ra and became king of the gods. With Mut and their son Khonsu, the moon, he formed the Theban triad. Every year in the Opet festival his statue sailed from Karnak to Luxor Temple. His priests grew so rich and powerful that Akhenaten closed his temples, and they reopened after Akhenaten's death.

Aten

Aton

The sun disk itself

Look for
No human form: a disk whose rays end in small hands, holding the ankh, the sign of life, to the royal family.
Home
Akhetaten (Amarna), the new capital Akhenaten built for him.

Akhenaten (c. 1353–1336 BC) made the Aten the one god of Egypt, closed the other temples and moved the court to Amarna. Temples to the Aten had no roof, so the sun could shine straight in. After his death Tutankhamun went back to Amun and the old gods, and later kings tried to erase the Amarna years from history.

Atum

Tem

The creator who made himself

Look for
A man wearing the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt.
Home
Heliopolis.

Before the world there was only Nun, dark endless water. Atum rose from it on the first mound of earth, the benben, and created the first pair of gods, Shu and Tefnut, on his own. Their descendants make up the Ennead, the nine gods of Heliopolis (see the family tree). The pyramidion on top of a pyramid and the tip of an obelisk echo that first mound.

Ptah

with Sekhmet and Nefertem, the triad of Memphis

Creator of Memphis, patron of craftsmen

Look for
A man wrapped like a mummy, with a close skullcap and a straight beard, holding a staff that joins the signs for power, stability and life.
Home
Memphis, the old capital south of Cairo.

In the Memphite version of creation, Ptah made the world by thinking it in his heart and speaking it with his tongue. The text survives on the Shabaka Stone, now in the British Museum. Builders, sculptors and goldsmiths were under his protection. His living image on earth was the Apis bull. At Abu Simbel he is the one god in the sanctuary whom the sun never lights.

Montu

Mentu

War god of Thebes

Look for
A falcon-headed man with two plumes and a sun disk.
Home
Armant (Iuny), Medamud and Tod near Thebes; a precinct at Karnak.

Montu was the great god of Thebes before Amun. The kings who reunited Egypt were named after him: Mentuhotep means "Montu is content". New Kingdom pharaohs went into battle as "a raging Montu".

The family of Osiris

Osiris

Usir · Wennefer, "the perfect one"

King of the dead, god of rebirth

Look for
A mummy-wrapped king with green or black skin, a tall white crown with plumes (the atef), holding the crook and the flail.
Home
Abydos, where his tomb was believed to lie.

Osiris ruled Egypt until his jealous brother Seth killed him and scattered his body. His wife Isis found the pieces, joined them into the first mummy and brought him back to life long enough to conceive their son Horus. Osiris became ruler of the underworld. Green and black are the colours of new crops and the Nile mud, so his death and return also stood for the flood and the harvest. Every dead person hoped to become "an Osiris".

Isis

Aset

Wife of Osiris, mother of Horus, great magician

Look for
A woman with a throne on her head, the sign that spells her name. Later she also wears cow horns with a sun disk, like Hathor.
Home
Philae, near Aswan.

Isis rebuilt Osiris and hid the baby Horus in the papyrus marshes of the Delta until he was old enough to fight Seth. She was the model of the loyal wife and protective mother, and her magic could heal. Her cult spread across the Greek and Roman world as far as Britain. Philae was the last working Egyptian temple, closed by the emperor Justinian around AD 537.

Seth

Set, Sutekh

Storms, desert and chaos

Look for
A man with the head of the "Seth animal", an unknown creature with a curved snout and square-tipped ears.
Home
Ombos (Naqada) in Upper Egypt, and Avaris in the Delta.

Seth murdered his brother Osiris and fought Horus for the throne for eighty years. Yet he was also strong and useful: standing at the prow of Ra's boat, he spears Apophis every night. The Hyksos rulers worshipped him at Avaris, and the Ramesside kings came from there, which is why Seti I's name means "man of Seth". In later periods he became purely the enemy.

Nephthys

Nebet-Hut, "lady of the house"

Protector of the dead

Look for
A woman with the signs for a house and a basket on her head, which spell her name.
Home
No major temple of her own; she appears beside Isis everywhere.

Seth's wife, but she sided with her sister Isis and mourned Osiris with her. The two sisters are shown as kites, birds of prey, at either end of royal coffins, spreading their wings over the dead. In later stories she is the mother of Anubis by Osiris.

Horus

Heru · Harsiese "son of Isis", Haroeris "the Elder"

The sky and kingship

Look for
A falcon, or a falcon-headed man wearing the double crown.
Home
Edfu; Hierakonpolis (Nekhen) in the earliest times.

The son of Isis and Osiris grew up to avenge his father and win the throne from Seth. In the fight Seth tore out his eye. Thoth healed it, and the wedjat, the Eye of Horus, became the most common protective amulet. Every living king was Horus on earth, and his first royal name was his "Horus name". At Kom Ombo an older form, Horus the Elder, shares the temple with Sobek.

Nut and Geb

with their parents Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture)

Sky and earth

Look for
Nut is a woman arching over the world, her body covered in stars. Geb lies beneath her, sometimes green with plants.
Home
Heliopolis, as part of the Ennead.

Shu, the air, holds his daughter Nut up and away from her husband Geb, the earth. Each evening Nut swallows the sun and each morning gives birth to it again, so her body stretches along the ceilings of royal tombs and coffin lids. Their children are Osiris, Isis, Seth and Nephthys.

Death and judgement

Anubis

Anpu, Inpu

Embalming and cemeteries

Look for
A black jackal lying on a shrine, or a man with a jackal head. Black is the colour of rebirth, not death.
Home
Cynopolis in Middle Egypt, and every necropolis.

Jackals prowled the desert cemeteries, so the Egyptians made a jackal their guardian. Anubis invented embalming for Osiris, and priests wearing his mask wrapped mummies. In the Hall of Judgement he guides the dead in and checks the scales. A carved Anubis on his shrine guarded the treasury in Tutankhamun's tomb.

Thoth

Djehuty · the Greeks' Hermes Trismegistus

Writing, wisdom and the moon

Look for
A man with the head of an ibis, or a baboon; often with a crescent moon and disk.
Home
Hermopolis (Khemenu) in Middle Egypt.

Thoth invented writing and counted time, so scribes prayed to him before starting work. He healed the Eye of Horus and settled the quarrel between Horus and Seth. In the Hall of Judgement he writes down the result of the weighing. Millions of mummified ibises and baboons were buried for him in the Late Period.

Ma'at

Maat

Truth, justice and cosmic order

Look for
A woman with a single ostrich feather on her head.
Home
Everywhere: Ma'at was an idea as much as a goddess.

Ma'at is the right order of things: the sun rising, the flood arriving, justice in court. The king's first duty was to uphold her, and temple walls show him offering a small figure of her to the gods. After death your heart was weighed against her feather. If it was heavier, laden with wrongdoing, the monster Ammit, part crocodile, lion and hippo, ate it.

The Nile, home and protection

Hathor

Hut-Hor, "house of Horus"

Love, music, joy and motherhood

Look for
A cow, or a woman with cow horns and a sun disk; on columns, a face with cow ears.
Home
Dendera.

Hathor was the goddess of music, dancing, drink and love, and a nurse of the king. On the west bank of Thebes she was "Lady of the West", who welcomed the dead. Her rattle, the sistrum, was shaken in her festivals, and the columns at Dendera carry her face. At Abu Simbel the small temple is hers, shared with Queen Nefertari.

Sekhmet

"The powerful one"

Lioness of war and plague, and healer

Look for
A woman with a lioness head and a sun disk.
Home
Memphis, as wife of Ptah.

When people rebelled against the ageing Ra, he sent his eye as Sekhmet to punish them, and she nearly wiped them out. To stop her, Ra flooded the fields with beer dyed red like blood. She drank it, fell asleep and woke as gentle Hathor. Her priests were also doctors. Amenhotep III set up hundreds of granite statues of her at Thebes.

Bastet

Bast

Home, women and cats

Look for
A cat, or a woman with a cat head, sometimes carrying a sistrum and a basket.
Home
Bubastis in the eastern Delta.

Bastet began as a fierce lioness and softened into a cat, protector of homes against snakes and evil. Herodotus describes her festival at Bubastis, with boatloads of singing pilgrims. Cats were so sacred to her that killing one could cost your life, and cats were mummified by the hundred thousand.

Sobek

Sebek

Crocodile god of the Nile

Look for
A crocodile, or a man with a crocodile head, crowned with plumes and a sun disk.
Home
The Faiyum (Crocodilopolis) and Kom Ombo.

Crocodiles were the most dangerous animals of the Nile, so the Egyptians worshipped one to stay safe and to share its strength. Sobek was linked to the fertility of the river banks and to royal power; the Middle Kingdom queen Sobekneferu was named after him. Sacred crocodiles were kept in pools and mummified when they died.

Khnum

Khnemu

The potter who shapes people

Look for
A man with the head of a ram with long, wavy horizontal horns.
Home
Elephantine Island at Aswan, and Esna.

Khnum shaped every child and its ka on his potter's wheel and placed them in the mother's womb. At Elephantine he guarded the caverns where the Nile was thought to rise. The Famine Stela on Sehel Island tells how Djoser ended seven years of famine by honouring him.

Hapi

Hapy

The Nile flood

Look for
A plump man with a belly and breasts, a sign of plenty, with papyrus or lotus plants on his head, carrying offerings.
Home
Gebel el-Silsila and the cataracts.

Hapi was not the river itself but the flood that came every summer and covered the fields with black silt. Without him there was famine. Hymns thanked him, and kings threw offerings into the river to make sure he returned. He is often shown in pairs, tying the plants of Upper and Lower Egypt together.

Taweret

"The great one"

Protector of childbirth

Look for
A pregnant hippopotamus standing upright, with lion paws and a crocodile tail, resting a hand on the sa sign of protection.
Home
Household shrines; no large temple.

The fierce mix of animals scared away anything that might harm a mother or a baby. Taweret appears on beds, headrests and amulets, and women prayed to her before giving birth. She was a goddess of homes rather than temples.

Bes

Protector of homes, children and fun

Look for
A bearded dwarf with a lion's mane, ears and tail, sticking out his tongue, with a crown of plumes. Unusually, he faces you head-on.
Home
Everywhere at home; birth houses (mammisi) of late temples.

Bes danced, played music and pulled faces to frighten demons and snakes away from sleeping children and women in labour. His image was painted on walls, carved on beds and worn as amulets. He looks frightening, but he was the friendliest god in the house.

Apis

Hapi (a different word from the flood god) · later Serapis

The sacred bull of Memphis

Look for
A black bull with a white triangle on the forehead and a sun disk between its horns.
Home
Memphis; buried at Saqqara.

One real bull at a time was Apis, the living image of Ptah, chosen by special markings. When it died it was mummified and buried in a huge granite sarcophagus in the Serapeum at Saqqara. The Ptolemies combined Osiris and Apis into Serapis, a god for both Greeks and Egyptians, whose great temple stood in Alexandria where Pompey's Pillar stands today.
