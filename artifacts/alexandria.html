Alexandria in a Day

Founded by Alexander the Great on the Mediterranean, Alexandria was for three hundred years the capital of the Ptolemies and the greatest city of the Greek world: the home of the Library, the Pharos lighthouse and Cleopatra. Most of the ancient city lies under the modern one or under the sea, so this guide follows our four stops, the Bibliotheca, Qaitbay, Pompey's Pillar and the catacombs, and shows what stood around them.

  1. 1

    Bibliotheca Alexandrina

    The new library, opened in 2002 near the site of the ancient one: a huge disc of glass and aluminium tilted towards the sea like a rising sun, above a granite wall carved with letters from scripts all over the world. Inside are a vast reading room on terraced floors, an antiquities museum and a manuscripts museum. Outside stands a colossal granite Ptolemy raised from the harbour.

    The ancient Library ↓

  2. 2

    The Mouseion and the Library

    Nothing is left of the Mouseion, the 'shrine of the Muses' where the Ptolemies paid scholars to live and work, or of its Library, the greatest of the ancient world. They stood in the royal quarter, the Brucheion, inland from the Eastern Harbour.

    Ptolemy I ↗ · Ptolemy II ↗ · The Library ↓

  3. 3

    The sunken royal quarter

    The palaces of the Ptolemies, the little island of Antirhodos and the harbour of Cleopatra slid into the sea through earthquakes and subsidence. Divers mapping the bay since the 1990s have found sphinxes, statues of priests and paved floors a few metres down. The Corniche runs along the shore above them.

    Cleopatra VII ↗

  4. 4

    The Heptastadion

    A causeway seven stadia long, about 1.2 km, joined the island of Pharos to the city and split the sea into two harbours. Sand built up on both sides over the centuries until it became the broad neck of land where the old quarters of Anfushi and Gomrok stand today.

  5. 5

    The Citadel of Qaitbay

    The Mamluk sultan Qaitbay built this fort in 1477–1479 on the tip of the old island, on the foundations of the Pharos and partly from its stones, to guard the coast against the Ottomans. Walk round the walls for the view back across the Eastern Harbour to the city.

    More on the citadel ↓ · The Pharos ↓

  6. 6

    The Pharos

    The lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders, stood here from about 280 BC: some 100 m tall, the highest building in the world after the pyramids of Giza. Earthquakes in the 14th century brought it down; its blocks and statues lie in the sea just off the fort.

    More on the Pharos ↓

  7. 7

    The tombs of Anfushi

    Rock-cut family tombs of the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC, a short walk from the fort, whose walls are painted to look like alabaster and marble blocks, with Egyptian gods beside Greek patterns. Open irregularly; ask Omarcel if there is time.

  8. 8

    The lost tomb of Alexander

    Ptolemy I seized Alexander's body on its way to Macedonia and brought it to Egypt; Ptolemy IV gave it a mausoleum, the Sema, where Caesar and Augustus came to see it. It vanished in late Roman times. Tradition places it near the crossing of the ancient main streets, by the Nabi Daniel mosque.

    Alexander ↗ · Ptolemy IV ↗

  9. 9

    Kom el-Dikka

    In the city centre, excavations have uncovered a small Roman theatre with rows of marble seats, Roman baths and some twenty lecture halls of a late Roman school, the last home of Alexandrian learning in the 5th and 6th centuries AD.

  10. 10

    The National Museum

    An Italian-style mansion of the 1920s holding a compact collection from every age of the city: pharaonic statues, Greek and Roman sculpture, finds from the sunken harbour, Coptic and Islamic objects. Good for an hour if the day allows.

  11. 11

    Pompey's Pillar and the Serapeum

    A column of red Aswan granite about 27 m high, put up in AD 297 for the emperor Diocletian; medieval travellers wrongly thought Pompey was buried under it. It stands on the hill of the Serapeum, the great temple of Serapis, with two granite sphinxes and the underground galleries that held the temple's library.

  12. 12

    The catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa

    Roman-period tombs of the 2nd century AD cut three levels deep into the rock, where Egyptian gods appear in Roman dress: the most striking sight of the day.

The Pharos

The lighthouse that gave its name to all the others.

Reconstruction from ancient descriptions and coins; the details are uncertain.

A wonder of the world

Begun under Ptolemy I and finished under Ptolemy II around 280 BC, it was dedicated by Sostratus of Cnidus 'to the saviour gods, for those who sail the sea'. Its fire, perhaps helped by bronze mirrors, could be seen far out at sea on a coast with no landmarks.

Three tiers

Ancient writers and coins show a tall square tower, an octagonal tier and a round lantern topped by a statue, perhaps Zeus or Poseidon. A ramp inside let animals carry fuel up the tower.

Every lighthouse is a pharos

The island's name became the word for a lighthouse in Greek, Latin and many modern languages: faro in Spanish and Italian, phare in French.

Brought down by earthquakes

Damaged several times, it was ruined by the earthquakes of 1303 and 1323. When Qaitbay built his fort in 1477 he used the fallen blocks.

Found under the sea

In 1994 divers led by Jean-Yves Empereur charted thousands of blocks off the fort: pieces of the tower, sphinxes, obelisks and colossal statues of a Ptolemy and a queen dressed as pharaoh and Isis. Some now stand outside the Bibliotheca and in the museums.

Seen from the fort

From the walls of Qaitbay, look north-east over the water: the ruins lie only 6 to 8 m down, a few hundred metres away.

The Citadel of Qaitbay

A Mamluk fortress built on the stones of the Pharos. Eight stops, from the gate to the walls over the sea; tap a number on the drawing.

Simplified drawings, not to scale; the plan is schematic.

  1. 1

    The gate

    You come in from the land side, along the road over the old Heptastadion, through the gate in the outer wall. The fort stands on the very tip of the old island of Pharos, with the sea on three sides.

  2. 2

    The outer walls

    A ring of thick limestone walls with round towers closes the whole headland. On the sea side, later rulers added firing positions for cannon. You can walk along much of the rampart.

  3. 3

    The inner enclosure

    Between the outer and inner walls stood the garrison's quarters: barracks, storerooms and stables, so the fort could hold out on its own in a siege.

  4. 4

    The keep

    In the middle rises the keep, a massive square tower of three floors, about 30 m on each side, with a round tower at each corner. Look at the walls: ancient red granite and marble columns are laid in them sideways, reused from older buildings, perhaps from the Pharos itself, to bind the masonry.

  5. 5

    The mosque

    The ground floor of the keep holds a small mosque, so the garrison could pray without leaving the fort. Its minaret once rose above the keep; it has not survived.

  6. 6

    The galleries

    On the first floor, corridors run round inside the thick walls, with narrow openings from which the defenders could shoot at anyone approaching.

  7. 7

    The sultan's hall

    At the top is a hall where Qaitbay could sit and watch the sea for ships, and there was a mill and an oven so the garrison could make bread during a siege.

  8. 8

    The walls over the sea

    Walk to the north side: the waves break against the rocks below. Look out to the north-east, where the ruins of the Pharos lie a few metres under the water, and back across the Eastern Harbour to the city.

Built against the Ottomans

Sultan Qaitbay visited Alexandria in 1477, when the Ottoman Turks threatened the coast, and ordered a fort on the ruins of the Pharos; it was ready in about two years. He also fortified Rosetta, where in 1799 French soldiers found the Rosetta Stone built into the walls of his fort there.

Five centuries of guns

The Ottomans garrisoned it after 1517, Napoleon's army took it in 1798 and Muhammad Ali strengthened it. On 11 July 1882 the British fleet bombarded Alexandria and badly damaged the fort; it was restored from 1904 onwards.

On the stones of the Pharos

The fort stands on the lighthouse's foundations, and blocks from the fallen tower went into its walls. The rest still lies offshore, where divers found it in 1994.

Inside the catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa

Egypt, Greece and Rome in one underground tomb, cut around the 2nd century AD, reaching roughly 35 m down in three levels — only the top two are open to visitors today. Eight stops on the way down; tap a number on the drawing.

Simplified section, not to scale.

  1. 1

    Down the spiral stair

    A staircase of shallow steps winds round a round shaft cut straight down into the rock. Light and air came down the shaft, and the dead were lowered down the middle on ropes, so the mourners never had to carry them round the turns.

  2. 2

    The rotunda

    The stair arrives in a domed round hall with a well-like opening in the middle and benches and niches round the walls: the meeting place of the tomb, from which the rooms of the first level open.

  3. 3

    The banquet hall

    A triclinium, the Roman dining room: three stone couches cut from the rock in a U, where relatives lay to eat a funeral meal on the day of the burial and on anniversaries. The dishes were left behind and broken, perhaps one reason why the hill was called the 'mound of shards'.

  4. 4

    The Hall of Caracalla

    A separate tomb, reached today through a hole broken in the wall. It held mixed human and animal bones. Early excavators linked it to Caracalla's AD 215 massacre of Alexandria's young men, but there is no real evidence for that; more recent study suggests many of the bones are racehorses, and that the chamber was dedicated to Nemesis, goddess of fortune and of sport. Its walls are painted with an Egyptian scene of Osiris being mummified and, beside it, the Greek myth of Hades carrying off Persephone: two ideas of death on one wall.

  5. 5

    The facade and the vestibule

    Down the stair to the second level stands the main tomb, built like a little Egyptian temple: two columns with Egyptian capitals, a winged sun disk and Horus falcons above. Inside the vestibule, in two niches, stand statues of the man and woman buried here, with stiff Egyptian bodies and Roman faces and hair. Beside the door, two bearded serpents in the double crown, the good spirit Agathodaimon, carry the staff of Hermes and the wand of Dionysus, with Medusa heads above them.

  6. 6

    The burial chamber

    Three niches, each holding a sarcophagus cut from the living rock and carved with Greek garlands, ox skulls and Medusa heads; their lids cannot be lifted, so the bodies were apparently slid in sideways from the passage behind the chamber, built into the design from the start. Above the central one, Anubis bends over a mummy on a lion bed between Horus and Thoth; in the side niches a pharaoh offers to the Apis bull under the wings of Isis. At the door, Anubis stands guard dressed as a Roman legionary with spear and shield, facing a second armed figure, half jackal and half serpent.

  7. 7

    The gallery of niches

    A corridor runs all round the burial chamber, lined with loculi, shelf-like niches in rows, each closed with a slab once a body was inside; with urns for cremated ashes, the complex had room for an estimated 300 or more burials. The tomb began as one wealthy family's and was later used by many people, probably a burial club, until about the 4th century.

  8. 8

    The flooded third level

    A last stair leads down to a third level, now under groundwater. You can see the water from the bottom of the stairs, but not go in.

A donkey's discovery

The catacombs were found in 1900, the story goes, when a donkey pulling a cart fell through the ground into the shaft. The name means 'mound of shards', after the broken pottery that covered the hill.

Three worlds in one tomb

The plan and the funeral meals are Roman, the garlands, Medusas and serpent staffs Greek, the gods and the hope of becoming Osiris Egyptian. Alexandrians of the 2nd century AD saw no contradiction: they used all three to protect their dead.

In the garden above

Before or after the descent, look round the grounds: stone sarcophagi and small painted tombs brought here from other parts of the city, among them the Tigrane tomb, with Egyptian gods painted in a Roman style.

Pompey's Pillar and the Serapeum hill

The tallest ancient column in Egypt, standing where Alexandria's greatest temple once stood. Eight stops; tap a number on the drawing.

Simplified drawings, not to scale with each other; positions on the plan are approximate.

  1. 1

    The hill of Rhakotis

    The site is the only real rise in the old city, in Rhakotis, the Egyptian quarter that was here before Alexander. The Ptolemies set their new god's temple on it, high above the streets; today the path from the ticket office climbs through its ruins.

  2. 2

    Pompey's Pillar

    About 27 m high with base and capital. The shaft alone is a single block of red Aswan granite some 20 m long and nearly 3 m thick at the bottom, weighing well over 200 tonnes, topped by a Corinthian capital. A Greek inscription on the west side of the base dedicates it to 'the most just emperor, the protector of Alexandria, Diocletian the invincible', set up by the prefect Postumus around AD 297, after Diocletian had put down a revolt in the city. A statue of the emperor probably stood on top.

  3. 3

    The sphinxes

    Two red granite sphinxes of the Ptolemaic period crouch near the column, survivors of the courts and avenues that once surrounded the sanctuary.

  4. 4

    The temple of Serapis

    Ptolemy III built the great temple around 240 BC, and the Romans rebuilt it on a grander scale in the 2nd century AD. Inside sat a colossal statue of Serapis, said to be by the Greek sculptor Bryaxis. Only foundations and cuttings in the rock are left.

  5. 5

    The foundation plaques

    Under the corners of the temple, excavators in the 1940s found foundation deposits: small plaques of gold, silver, bronze, faience and other materials, inscribed in Greek and hieroglyphs, saying that Ptolemy III built the Serapeum. That is how we know who built it.

  6. 6

    The underground galleries

    Long corridors cut into the rock, lined with niches. They may have held the Serapeum's 'daughter library' or served the cult; their exact use is debated. Bring a light.

  7. 7

    The Apis bull

    In the galleries stands a copy of a black stone statue of the Apis bull, dedicated in Hadrian's reign in the 2nd century AD and found here in 1895; the original is in the Graeco-Roman Museum. Apis, the sacred bull of Memphis, was part of who Serapis was.

  8. 8

    The nilometer

    At the edge of the hill a stair goes down to the water, thought to have measured the yearly rise of the Nile flood: a reminder that even in this Greek city the river ruled.

Why 'Pompey'?

Pompey the Great was murdered on the Egyptian shore in 48 BC. Medieval travellers believed his head had been kept in an urn on top of the column, and the name stuck, though the column was raised 350 years after his death.

The end of the Serapeum

In AD 391, with the emperor Theodosius's approval, Christians led by the bishop Theophilus stormed the temple. A soldier struck the great statue of Serapis with an axe; when no disaster followed, the crowd tore it down, and the temple with it. Only the column was left standing.

Two sites, one afternoon

The catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa are about 500 m to the south-west, a short walk or drive: the two together make the afternoon.

Serapis and the Serapeum

What ancient writers saw in the greatest temple of Alexandria, and what it meant to the city.

A god for Greeks and Egyptians

Ptolemy I promoted Serapis, a new god joining Osiris-Apis, the dead Apis bull of Memphis, to the look of a Greek Zeus: bearded, with a corn measure on his head.

The Serapeum

Ptolemy III built him a great temple on this hill, later rebuilt by the Romans. Its galleries held a 'daughter library', and a statue of the Apis bull from Hadrian's time was found here.

The end of the temple

In AD 391, under the emperor Theodosius, Christians led by the bishop Theophilus pulled the Serapeum down, a moment often taken as the end of the ancient world in Alexandria.

Two temples, one site

The first Serapeum was Ptolemy III's, around 240 BC; Ptolemy IV added a shrine for Harpocrates, the child Horus. In the 2nd century AD the Romans rebuilt it far larger, as a temple in a vast colonnaded court, with Pompey's Pillar later raised inside it.

A hundred steps up

Rufinus, a Christian writer who saw it before 391, describes the temple raised on an artificial height and reached by about a hundred steps, with vaulted halls and passages underneath and porticoes, rooms and lecture halls all round.

The great statue

The cult statue, said to be by the Greek sculptor Bryaxis, was so large that its outstretched hands touched the walls on either side. It was made of wood and many metals, dark in colour, with a corn measure on its head and the three-headed dog Cerberus at its side.

The kiss of the sun

On the god's feast day, Rufinus says, a small window was opened so that a ray of sunlight fell on the statue's lips: the Sun coming to greet Serapis, a piece of temple theatre the crowds loved.

God of healing and the dead

Serapis healed the sick, who slept in his temples hoping for a cure in a dream, and ruled the afterlife like Osiris. With Isis, his cult spread across the whole Mediterranean, from Delos and Ephesus to Rome.

The Nile cubit

The rod used to measure the height of the Nile flood was kept in the Serapeum, until the emperor Constantine had it moved to a church. When the temple fell in 391, pagans feared the river would not rise; it rose as usual.

The ancient Library

All the books in the world

Founded under Ptolemy I and Ptolemy II, around 290–280 BC, the Library tried to own every Greek book. Ships in the harbour, it was said, had their books copied and the copies given back.

The scholars

Euclid wrote his geometry here, Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth, Callimachus made the first library catalogue, and Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Bible into Greek, the Septuagint.

Not one fire

The Library did not end in a single blaze. Caesar's fire in 48 BC burned books near the harbour, scholars were driven out under Ptolemy VIII, the royal quarter was wrecked in the 270s AD, and the Serapeum was destroyed in AD 391. The tale of a caliph burning it in 642 is a later legend.

Cleopatra's city

Alexandria was the capital of the Ptolemies for three centuries. Cleopatra VII lived in its palaces with Caesar and then Antony, and died here in 30 BC, when Egypt became a Roman province.

A city that nearly disappeared — and came back

Ancient Alexandria's glory had a long, little-known epilogue: a thousand years of decline, then two separate rebirths.

A shrinking port

After the Arab conquest of 641, Alexandria stopped being Egypt's capital — the new rulers founded Fustat, later Cairo, further up the Nile. Its harbours silted up and earthquakes and plague wore the city down for a thousand years; by the time Napoleon's fleet anchored offshore in 1798, the old metropolis had shrunk to a fishing town of barely 10,000 people, huddled on the old Heptastadion peninsula.

Muhammad Ali's canal

The revival began with a brutal public-works project: between 1817 and 1820, the new ruler Muhammad Ali had tens of thousands of peasants dig the Mahmoudiya Canal, finally reconnecting Alexandria to the Nile at the delta village of al-Atf. The canal made the city Egypt's gateway to the Mediterranean again, and the population exploded — by the First World War it was nearing half a million.

A cosmopolitan city

The boom drew in Greek, Italian, Jewish, Armenian and Levantine communities, who built banks, theatres and newspapers in half a dozen languages. The poet Constantine Cavafy and the novelists E. M. Forster and Lawrence Durrell, whose Alexandria Quartet is set here, all wrote of this vanished, mixed-up city.

The cosmopolitan city ends

The 1956 Suez Crisis broke it apart: Egypt expelled around 25,000 Jewish residents holding foreign passports, and nationalisations through the early 1960s pushed most of the Greek and Italian communities to leave too. Today's Alexandria, Egypt's second city and a major Mediterranean port, carries only traces of that vanished world.

Alexandria through time

  1. 331 BC

    Alexander the Great founds the city on a strip of land between the sea and Lake Mariout.

  2. 323–283 BC

    Ptolemy I makes Alexandria the capital and brings Alexander's body here; the Mouseion and Library are founded.

For our visit

An early start

The drive from Cairo takes about three hours each way, so leave early and keep the day to the four main stops.

The Bibliotheca is closed some days

It usually closes on Fridays and holidays; Wednesday is fine. Check bag rules at the entrance.

Catacombs: no photos?

Photography rules underground change; ask at the ticket office. The stairs are steep and the air is humid.

Lunch by the sea

Alexandria is famous for its fish: a fish restaurant near Qaitbay or on the Corniche fits between the morning and afternoon stops.
