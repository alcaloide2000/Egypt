Alexandria in a Day

Founded by Alexander the Great on the Mediterranean, Alexandria was for three hundred years the capital of the Ptolemies and the greatest city of the Greek world: the home of the Library, the Pharos lighthouse and Cleopatra. Most of the ancient city lies under the modern one or under the sea, so this guide follows our four stops, the Bibliotheca, Qaitbay, Pompey's Pillar and the catacombs, and shows what stood around them.

Around the city

Twelve stops, from the Bibliotheca along the harbour to Qaitbay, through the centre and out to the Serapeum hill and the catacombs. Some places have vanished, under the sea or under the modern city. Tap a number on the map.

  1. 1

    Bibliotheca Alexandrina

    The new library, opened in 2002 near the site of the ancient one: a huge disc of glass and aluminium tilted towards the sea like a rising sun, above a granite wall carved with letters from scripts all over the world. Inside are a vast reading room on terraced floors, an antiquities museum and a manuscripts museum. Outside stands a colossal granite Ptolemy raised from the harbour.

    The ancient Library ↓

  2. 2

    The Mouseion and the Library

    Nothing is left of the Mouseion, the 'shrine of the Muses' where the Ptolemies paid scholars to live and work, or of its Library, the greatest of the ancient world. They stood in the royal quarter, the Brucheion, inland from the Eastern Harbour.

    Ptolemy I ↗ · Ptolemy II ↗ · The Library ↓

  3. 3

    The sunken royal quarter

    The palaces of the Ptolemies, the little island of Antirhodos and the harbour of Cleopatra slid into the sea through earthquakes and subsidence. Divers mapping the bay since the 1990s have found sphinxes, statues of priests and paved floors a few metres down. The Corniche runs along the shore above them.

    Cleopatra VII ↗

  4. 4

    The Heptastadion

    A causeway seven stadia long, about 1.2 km, joined the island of Pharos to the city and split the sea into two harbours. Sand built up on both sides over the centuries until it became the broad neck of land where the old quarters of Anfushi and Gomrok stand today.

  5. 5

    The Citadel of Qaitbay

    The Mamluk sultan Qaitbay built this fort in 1477–1479 on the tip of the old island, on the foundations of the Pharos and partly from its stones, to guard the coast against the Ottomans. Walk round the walls for the view back across the Eastern Harbour to the city.

    The Pharos ↓

  6. 6

    The Pharos

    The lighthouse of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders, stood here from about 280 BC: some 100 m tall, the highest building in the world after the pyramids of Giza. Earthquakes in the 14th century brought it down; its blocks and statues lie in the sea just off the fort.

    More on the Pharos ↓

  7. 7

    The tombs of Anfushi

    Rock-cut family tombs of the 3rd and 2nd centuries BC, a short walk from the fort, whose walls are painted to look like alabaster and marble blocks, with Egyptian gods beside Greek patterns. Open irregularly; ask Omarcel if there is time.

  8. 8

    The lost tomb of Alexander

    Ptolemy I seized Alexander's body on its way to Macedonia and brought it to Egypt; Ptolemy IV gave it a mausoleum, the Sema, where Caesar and Augustus came to see it. It vanished in late Roman times. Tradition places it near the crossing of the ancient main streets, by the Nabi Daniel mosque.

    Alexander ↗ · Ptolemy IV ↗

  9. 9

    Kom el-Dikka

    In the city centre, excavations have uncovered a small Roman theatre with rows of marble seats, Roman baths and some twenty lecture halls of a late Roman school, the last home of Alexandrian learning in the 5th and 6th centuries AD.

  10. 10

    The National Museum

    An Italian-style mansion of the 1920s holding a compact collection from every age of the city: pharaonic statues, Greek and Roman sculpture, finds from the sunken harbour, Coptic and Islamic objects. Good for an hour if the day allows.

  11. 11

    Pompey's Pillar and the Serapeum

    A column of red Aswan granite about 27 m high, put up in AD 297 for the emperor Diocletian; medieval travellers wrongly thought Pompey was buried under it. It stands on the hill of the Serapeum, the great temple of Serapis, with two granite sphinxes and the underground galleries that held the temple's library.

    Ptolemy III ↗ · Serapis ↓

  12. 12

    The catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa

    Roman-period tombs of the 2nd century AD cut three levels deep into the rock, where Egyptian gods appear in Roman dress: the most striking sight of the day.

The Pharos

The lighthouse that gave its name to all the others.

Reconstruction from ancient descriptions and coins; the details are uncertain.

A wonder of the world

Begun under Ptolemy I and finished under Ptolemy II around 280 BC, it was dedicated by Sostratus of Cnidus 'to the saviour gods, for those who sail the sea'. Its fire, perhaps helped by bronze mirrors, could be seen far out at sea on a coast with no landmarks.

Three tiers

Ancient writers and coins show a tall square tower, an octagonal tier and a round lantern topped by a statue, perhaps Zeus or Poseidon. A ramp inside let animals carry fuel up the tower.

Every lighthouse is a pharos

The island's name became the word for a lighthouse in Greek, Latin and many modern languages: faro in Spanish and Italian, phare in French.

Brought down by earthquakes

Damaged several times, it was ruined by the earthquakes of 1303 and 1323. When Qaitbay built his fort in 1477 he used the fallen blocks.

Found under the sea

In 1994 divers led by Jean-Yves Empereur charted thousands of blocks off the fort: pieces of the tower, sphinxes, obelisks and colossal statues of a Ptolemy and a queen dressed as pharaoh and Isis. Some now stand outside the Bibliotheca and in the museums.

Seen from the fort

From the walls of Qaitbay, look north-east over the water: the ruins lie only 6 to 8 m down, a few hundred metres away.

Inside the catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa

Egypt, Greece and Rome in one underground tomb, cut around the 2nd century AD.

Simplified section, not to scale.

A donkey's discovery

The catacombs were found in 1900, the story goes, when a donkey pulling a cart fell through the ground into the shaft. The name means 'mound of shards', after the broken pottery that covered the hill.

Down the spiral stair

A staircase winds round a round shaft; the dead were lowered down the middle on ropes. Its first landing leads to a domed rotunda and to the banquet hall, where relatives ate funeral meals on stone benches.

Two worlds on one facade

The main tomb has an Egyptian front, with papyrus columns, a winged sun disk and Horus falcons, but Greek touches; two bearded serpents, the good spirit Agathodaimon, wear the double crown and guard the door.

Anubis the legionary

Beside the inner door, Anubis and a second jackal-headed figure stand in the armour and cloak of Roman soldiers. Inside, the dead man and woman are carved with Egyptian poses and Roman faces, and Anubis, Thoth and Horus tend a mummy on a lion bed.

Hundreds of niches

Round the burial chamber runs a corridor with rows of shelf-like niches, loculi, for later burials. The tomb was used for generations, probably by a burial club as well as one family.

The Hall of Caracalla

A neighbouring tomb, reached through a hole in the wall, held the bones of horses and young men. It is linked, without proof, to the massacre of Alexandria's youth by the emperor Caracalla in AD 215.

Serapis and the Serapeum

A god for Greeks and Egyptians

Ptolemy I promoted Serapis, a new god joining Osiris-Apis, the dead Apis bull of Memphis, to the look of a Greek Zeus: bearded, with a corn measure on his head.

The Serapeum

Ptolemy III built him a great temple on this hill, later rebuilt by the Romans. Its galleries held a 'daughter library', and a statue of the Apis bull from Hadrian's time was found here.

The end of the temple

In AD 391, under the emperor Theodosius, Christians led by the bishop Theophilus pulled the Serapeum down, a moment often taken as the end of the ancient world in Alexandria.

The ancient Library

All the books in the world

Founded under Ptolemy I and Ptolemy II, around 290–280 BC, the Library tried to own every Greek book. Ships in the harbour, it was said, had their books copied and the copies given back.

The scholars

Euclid wrote his geometry here, Eratosthenes measured the size of the Earth, Callimachus made the first library catalogue, and Jewish scholars translated the Hebrew Bible into Greek, the Septuagint.

Not one fire

The Library did not end in a single blaze. Caesar's fire in 48 BC burned books near the harbour, scholars were driven out under Ptolemy VIII, the royal quarter was wrecked in the 270s AD, and the Serapeum was destroyed in AD 391. The tale of a caliph burning it in 642 is a later legend.

Cleopatra's city

Alexandria was the capital of the Ptolemies for three centuries. Cleopatra VII lived in its palaces with Caesar and then Antony, and died here in 30 BC, when Egypt became a Roman province.

Alexandria through time

  1. 331 BC

    Alexander the Great founds the city on a strip of land between the sea and Lake Mariout.

  2. 323–283 BC

    Ptolemy I makes Alexandria the capital and brings Alexander's body here; the Mouseion and Library are founded.

  3. c. 280 BC

    The Pharos is finished under Ptolemy II.

  4. c. 240 BC

    Ptolemy III builds the Serapeum; Eratosthenes measures the Earth.

  5. 48–30 BC

    Caesar, then Antony, with Cleopatra VII; she dies in 30 BC and Egypt becomes Roman.

  6. 2nd century AD

    The catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa are cut.

  7. AD 297

    Pompey's Pillar is raised for Diocletian.

  8. AD 391

    The Serapeum is destroyed.

  9. AD 641–642

    The Arab conquest; Fustat, later Cairo, becomes the capital.

  10. 1303 and 1323

    Earthquakes ruin the Pharos.

  11. 1477–1479

    Sultan Qaitbay builds his fort on its site.

  12. 1900

    The catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa are found.

  13. 1994

    Divers chart the ruins of the Pharos off the fort.

  14. 2002

    The Bibliotheca Alexandrina opens.

For our visit

An early start

The drive from Cairo takes about three hours each way, so leave early and keep the day to the four main stops.

The Bibliotheca is closed some days

It usually closes on Fridays and holidays; Wednesday is fine. Check bag rules at the entrance.

Catacombs: no photos?

Photography rules underground change; ask at the ticket office. The stairs are steep and the air is humid.

Lunch by the sea

Alexandria is famous for its fish: a fish restaurant near Qaitbay or on the Corniche fits between the morning and afternoon stops.
