Philae, island of Isis

On a small island above the First Cataract, the last great temple of ancient Egypt was built for Isis, the great mother and magician, mostly by the Ptolemies and Roman emperors. Pilgrims came here from Egypt, Nubia and across the Mediterranean, and the old religion lived on at Philae for more than a century after it had died everywhere else. Between 1977 and 1980 every block was moved to Agilkia, a neighbouring island reshaped to look like the original.

Walking the island, south to north

The visit follows the route of the old processions: from the landing at the southern tip, along the colonnades, through two pylons and the columned hall to the sanctuary. Tap a number on the plan.

Simplified plan of Agilkia Island with approximate positions. North is up; the pink dashed line is the processional route.

  1. 1

    The landing and the Kiosk of Nectanebo I

    Boats tie up at the southern tip. The small gateway-hall here, with columns crowned by the face of Hathor, was built by Nectanebo I around 370 BC: it is the oldest building standing on the island.

    Nectanebo I ↗

  2. 2

    The outer court

    A long, slightly wedge-shaped court leads north. The west colonnade, with dozens of columns whose capitals are all different, was decorated under the Roman emperors Augustus and Tiberius; its windows looked out to Bigeh. The east colonnade was never finished.

  3. 3

    Small temples on the east side

    Along the east side stand small shrines to Arensnuphis, a Nubian god, to Mandulis, the god of Kalabsha, and to Imhotep, Djoser's architect, worshipped here as a god of healing. Philae was holy to Egyptians and Nubians alike.

    Djoser and Imhotep ↗

  4. 4

    The first pylon

    The great gateway, about 18 m high, shows Ptolemy XII smiting enemies before Isis, Horus and Hathor. Inside the doorway, French soldiers of Napoleon's expedition carved a dated inscription in 1799.

    Ptolemy XII ↗

  5. 5

    The birth house

    On the west of the forecourt stands the mammisi, where the birth of Horus was celebrated: Isis gave birth to him in the papyrus marshes and hid him from Seth. Its columns carry Hathor heads, and its walls show Isis nursing the child.

    Horus ↗

  6. 6

    The second pylon

    The second gateway is set at an angle to the first: the builders had to follow the shape of the rock and older buildings. Look for Ptolemy XII again, offering to the gods.

  7. 7

    The hypostyle hall

    Ten columns with flower capitals still keep some of their colours. When the temple became a church in the 6th century, Christians carved crosses into the walls and chiselled the faces off many figures, and the hall served as the church of St Stephen.

  8. 8

    The sanctuary of Isis

    The innermost room is small and dark. Its granite stand once held the boat-shrine of Isis's statue; it was dedicated by Ptolemy III and his queen Berenice II. Isis's cult continued here longer than anywhere else in Egypt.

    Isis ↗ · Ptolemy III ↗

  9. 9

    The Gate of Hadrian

    West of the temple, a Roman gateway shows Hapi, the god of the flood, crouching in a cave under the rocks of Bigeh with a snake coiled round him, pouring out the Nile. Here too is the last dated hieroglyphic inscription ever carved, on 24 August AD 394.

    Hapi ↗

  10. 10

    The Temple of Hathor

    A small temple east of the main one, begun under Ptolemy VI and VIII. Its reliefs show the god Bes and other musicians playing harps, drums and tambourines for the goddess of music and joy.

    Hathor ↗ · Bes ↗

  11. 11

    The Kiosk of Trajan

    The island's most photographed building: fourteen columns joined by screen walls, on the east shore, built under the Roman emperor Trajan (AD 98–117) as a grand entrance from the river. It was never finished, and its roof was probably of wood. Visitors nicknamed it 'Pharaoh's Bed'.

  12. 12

    Towards Bigeh

    Across the water lies Bigeh, the Abaton, believed to hold the tomb of Osiris. Only priests could set foot there; every ten days the statue of Isis was taken across by boat to pour milk offerings for her husband.

    Osiris ↗

Drowned and saved

The first Aswan Dam, finished in 1902, flooded Philae for most of the year: for seventy years visitors rowed between the columns. The High Dam would have drowned it for good, so between 1972 and 1980 a UNESCO team built a cofferdam round the island, pumped it dry, cut the buildings into some 40,000 blocks and rebuilt them on Agilkia, a few hundred metres away, reshaped to match Philae's outline and orientation.

Philae through time

  1. c. 690–664 BC

    Blocks of the Kushite pharaoh Taharqa show there was already a shrine on the island.

  2. c. 370 BC

    Nectanebo I, of the last native dynasty, builds the gateway-kiosk at the south end.

  3. c. 280–250 BC

    Ptolemy II begins the present Temple of Isis; his successors add the birth house, the pylons and the decoration.

  4. c. 80–51 BC

    Ptolemy XII, Cleopatra's father, decorates the great first pylon.

  5. AD 1st–2nd c.

    Roman emperors add the colonnades, the Gate of Hadrian and the Kiosk of Trajan.

  6. 24 Aug 394

    The last dated hieroglyphic inscription is carved, on the Gate of Hadrian.

  7. c. 537

    Justinian has the temple closed; the hypostyle hall becomes a church.

  8. 1799

    French troops of Napoleon's expedition reach Philae and carve their inscription in the first pylon.

  9. 1902

    The first Aswan Dam floods the island for most of the year.

  10. 1972–1980

    UNESCO rescue: cofferdam, dismantling in 1977, rebuilding on Agilkia, reopening in March 1980.

Inside the doorway of the first pylon, look for the inscription carved by Napoleon's soldiers in 1799.

The Nile's source
