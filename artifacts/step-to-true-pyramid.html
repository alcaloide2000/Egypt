Old Kingdom · c. 2670–2510 BC

From Step to True Pyramid

In about eighty years, Egypt's builders went from Djoser's stairway of stone at Saqqara to Sneferu's smooth-sided Red Pyramid at Dahshur, each pyramid fixing a problem with the one before it. Sneferu's son, grandson and great-grandson then took what they had learned to Giza. All drawings use the same scale, from the real measurements.

The angle problem

The outlines placed on top of each other. Meidum tried about 52° and failed; the Bent Pyramid started steeper still and backed off to the Red Pyramid's safe 43.4°. On the solid rock of Giza, Khufu and Khafre could go back to 52–53°. Menkaure, at 51.3°, repeats Khufu's angle at a much smaller size, so it is left out here.

Learning to build a pyramid

1Saqqara · Dynasty 3 · c. 2670–2650 BC

Step Pyramid of Djoser

Height
62.5 m
Base
121 × 109 m
Form
six steps

Djoser's architect Imhotep built Egypt's first monument entirely in stone. It began as a square mastaba, a flat-topped tomb, and grew in stages to six steps, each one a smaller mastaba set on the one below. The stones lean inward in layers, which keeps the stack stable.

The idea: stack mastabas into a stairway to the sky.

2Meidum · end of Dynasty 3 to Dynasty 4 · c. 2600 BC

Pyramid of Meidum

Planned
c. 92 m, base 144 m
Today
c. 65 m tower
Angle
c. 52°

Probably begun for Huni as a step pyramid of seven, then eight, steps. Sneferu later filled in the steps and added a smooth outer casing, making the first attempt at a true pyramid. The casing rested on sand rather than rock and was not bonded to the core. It later fell away, leaving the stepped tower standing on its own rubble. When it collapsed is still debated.

The lesson: a smooth skin needs a solid footing and a bonded core.

3Dahshur · Sneferu, Dynasty 4 · c. 2600 BC

Bent Pyramid

Height
101 m
Base
188.6 m
Angle
54.5°, then 43.4°

Designed as a true pyramid from the start, at a steep 54.5°. The soft clay ground below began to settle and the chambers cracked, so the builders changed to a gentler 43.4° at about 47 m, which gave the pyramid its bend. Much of its fine white limestone casing still survives.

The fix: lower the angle and reduce the weight.

4Dahshur · Sneferu, Dynasty 4 · c. 2590s BC

Red Pyramid

Height
104 m
Base
220 m
Angle
43.4°

Sneferu's third pyramid used the safer 43.4° from the base up and stands on firmer ground. It worked: the first successful smooth-sided pyramid, named for the reddish limestone of its core, now that its white casing is gone. It was probably Sneferu's tomb. A generation later his son Khufu built the Great Pyramid at Giza at a steeper 51.8°, on solid rock.

The result: the first true pyramid, and the model for Giza.

Giza: the pyramid perfected

5Giza · Dynasty 4 · c. 2580–2560 BC

Great Pyramid of Khufu

Height
146.6 m, now 138.5 m
Base
230.3 m
Angle
51.8°

Sneferu's son took the lessons of Dahshur to the Giza plateau, where the pyramid could stand on solid limestone bedrock. That made the steep angle Meidum had tried safe at last. About 2.3 million blocks went into it, and its sides face north, south, east and west to within a fraction of a degree. Papyri found at Wadi al-Jarf, the diary of an official called Merer, record boats bringing casing stone from Tura in Khufu's 27th year. It stayed the tallest building in the world for about 3,800 years.

The peak: solid rock makes the steep angle safe.

6Giza · Dynasty 4 · c. 2558–2532 BC

Pyramid of Khafre

Height
143.5 m, now 136.4 m
Base
215.3 m
Angle
53.1°

Khufu's son built slightly smaller but steeper, on rock about 10 m higher, so from many viewpoints it looks taller than his father's. It is the only Giza pyramid that keeps some of its smooth limestone casing, near the top. Its causeway runs down to the valley temple beside the Great Sphinx, carved in his reign.

The look: steeper and on higher ground, with the Sphinx below.

7Giza · Dynasty 4 · c. 2510 BC

Pyramid of Menkaure

Height
65.5 m
Base
102.2 × 104.6 m
Angle
51.3°

Less than a tenth of Khufu's volume, but the most lavish casing: its lowest sixteen courses were red granite brought from Aswan, more than 800 km upriver. The granite was never fully smoothed, a sign the work stopped at his death. His son Shepseskaf finished the temples in mud brick and built no pyramid at all.

The end of an era: after Menkaure, pyramids grew smaller.
