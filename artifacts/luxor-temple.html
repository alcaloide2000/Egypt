Luxor Temple

In the middle of modern Luxor, by the river, stands the temple that Amun visited once a year from Karnak, 2.7 km to the north. Amenhotep III built most of it, Ramesses II gave it its great gateway, and Alexander the Great, the Romans, Christians and Muslims all left their mark. It is open into the evening, when the floodlit columns are at their most beautiful.

From the avenue to the sanctuary

Twelve stops, walking south from the avenue of sphinxes into ever older and holier rooms. Tap a number on the plan.

  1. 1

    The avenue of sphinxes

    A processional road about 2.7 km long joined Luxor to Karnak, the road of the gods at the Opet festival. More than a thousand sphinxes lined it: near Luxor they have lions' bodies and the face of Nectanebo I, who set most of them up around 370 BC; near Karnak and the temple of Mut many have rams' heads. Buried under the town for centuries, it was dug out over decades and reopened on 25 November 2021.

  2. 2

    The First Pylon and the obelisk

    Ramesses II's gateway, about 65 m wide, carved with his battle of Kadesh against the Hittites. In front stands one red granite obelisk, about 25 m high; its twin was given to France and has stood in the Place de la Concorde in Paris since 1836.

    Ramesses II ↗

  3. 3

    The colossi of Ramesses II

    Six giant statues of Ramesses II once stood before the pylon: two seated on either side of the gate and four standing. The two seated ones, in granite and about 15 m high with their crowns, are still in place, with one standing figure; the rest are fragments. On the sides of the thrones Nile gods tie the plants of the Two Lands, as at the Colossi of Memnon.

    Ramesses II ↗ · The colossi in detail ↓

  4. 4

    The court of Ramesses II

    A court of 74 papyrus columns with statues of the king between them. Look at the plan: it is set at an angle to the older temple, perhaps to line up with the avenue to Karnak. In the north-west corner Ramesses kept an older triple shrine of Hatshepsut and Thutmose III for the boats of Amun, Mut and Khonsu.

    Thutmose III ↗

  5. 5

    The mosque of Abu el-Haggag

    Built in the 13th century on the sand and rubble that filled the court, in honour of a Sufi saint of Luxor, and still in use. Its old door hangs high on the wall: that was the ground level before the temple was dug out in the 1880s.

  6. 6

    The colonnade of Amenhotep III

    Fourteen giant columns in two rows of seven, the tallest in the temple, with open papyrus capitals still carrying their stone beams. Amenhotep III built them as the grand entrance of his temple, perhaps as the nave of a huge hall he never finished. Walls once closed the sides and a roof covered it, making a tall dark passage; the walls were carved under Tutankhamun with the Opet festival procession, and later taken over by Horemheb.

    Amenhotep III ↗ · Tutankhamun ↗ · The colonnade in detail ↓ · The Opet festival ↓

  7. 7

    The sun court of Amenhotep III

    A vast court surrounded on three sides by double rows of papyrus-bundle columns, among the most elegant in Egypt. In 1989 workers found here a hidden pit with 26 fine statues, the Luxor cachette, now in the Luxor Museum.

  8. 8

    The hypostyle hall

    A hall of 32 columns, the entrance to the inner temple. From here on the rooms get smaller, darker and holier.

  9. 9

    The Roman chapel

    Around AD 300 the temple was inside a Roman fort, and this room became a shrine to the emperors: an old doorway was walled into an apse, and the walls were painted with the Roman tetrarchs and their court. The frescoes have been cleaned and are visible again over the older reliefs.

  10. 10

    The shrine of Alexander the Great

    In the room where Amun's boat rested, a barque shrine was rebuilt in the name of Alexander the Great, around 330 BC. Its walls show the Macedonian conqueror as a pharaoh, offering to Amun.

    Alexander ↗

  11. 11

    The birth room

    To the east, reliefs tell how Amenhotep III was born of the god: Amun visits his mother Mutemwia, and the potter god Khnum shapes the child on his wheel, as Hatshepsut had shown at Deir el-Bahari.

    Khnum ↗

  12. 12

    The sanctuary of Amenhotep III

    At the far end, the holy of holies where the statue of Amun of Luxor stood. It is the oldest and most hidden part of the temple.

    Amun ↗

The avenue of sphinxes

The road that gave Luxor Temple its meaning: every year the gods walked it, and today you can too.

Schematic route, not to scale.

The way of the god

For the Egyptians this was a sacred road, built for the yearly journey of Amun, Mut and Khonsu from Karnak to Luxor. Priests carried the gods' gilded boats on their shoulders, between rows of sphinxes, while crowds watched, sang and feasted.

Way stations for the boats

Long before the sphinxes, Hatshepsut built six small chapels along the route where the priests could set the boats down and rest; her Red Chapel at Karnak shows them. The avenue we see is the last and grandest version of a road at least a thousand years old.

More than a thousand sphinxes

There were once well over a thousand, one every few metres on each side, each on a plinth carved with the king's name. Most are broken or headless now, and many were carried off as building stone, but long rows have been put back in place.

Nectanebo's faces

The human-headed sphinxes near Luxor have the body of a lion and the face of Nectanebo I, one of the last native pharaohs, around 370 BC: by lining the gods' road with his own image, he placed himself among the great kings of Thebes.

Buried and found again

Over the centuries the road disappeared under silt, houses and streets. The first sphinxes in front of Luxor Temple were found in 1949; from the 2000s whole stretches were cleared, and the avenue reopened on 25 November 2021 with a great night parade recalling the Opet festival.

What to look for

The plinths with Nectanebo's cartouches, the rams near Karnak, and along the way the remains of later Roman workshops, including presses for making wine, built when the road was no longer sacred.

The colossi of Ramesses II

Ramesses II, who ruled for 66 years, filled Egypt with images of himself, and Luxor Temple has some of the finest.

Simplified front view of the First Pylon, not to scale; lost statues and the missing obelisk are dashed.

Six giants at the gate

Two seated colossi flanked the doorway and four standing ones stood further out, two on each side, all of Ramesses II. Earthquakes and stone robbers broke most of them; the two seated statues survive, with one standing figure, and fragments of the others lie at their feet.

The seated kings

Each sits on a block throne, hands on knees, wearing the nemes headcloth with the cobra on the brow and, above it, the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. With their crowns they rise about 15 m, as high as a five-storey building.

The thrones

On the sides of the thrones, two Nile gods tie the lily of the south and the papyrus of the north round the hieroglyph for 'unite'. The king's names are carved on the backs and bases, large enough to be read from the avenue.

Behind them, the battle

The pylon towers behind the statues tell the battle of Kadesh, fought against the Hittites in Syria around 1274 BC: the camp surprised, and Ramesses charging alone in his chariot. In fact the battle ended in a draw, and later in the first peace treaty known.

Nefertari at his feet

More statues of Ramesses stand between the columns of his court. At its far end, by the entrance to the colonnade, a seated colossus has his great royal wife Nefertari, small and graceful, standing beside his leg: the same queen of the Small Temple at Abu Simbel.

Borrowed statues

Some of the court's statues may first have been carved for Amenhotep III and later given Ramesses's names and features. Ramesses often re-used older monuments: look for cartouches that seem cut deeper or recut over older signs.

The colonnade of Amenhotep III

A processional corridor of giant columns, and the best picture of an Egyptian festival that has come down to us.

Simplified side view of one row of columns, not to scale; the lost roof is dashed.

A hall that was never finished

Amenhotep III built the colonnade around 1360 BC as the way in to his temple. Many think it was meant to be only the central nave of a vast hypostyle hall, like Karnak's, with lower columns on each side; when he died, only the nave had been raised.

Giant papyrus

Each smooth shaft ends in an open papyrus flower, the plant of the primeval marsh. The stone beams above still carry Amenhotep III's names. With side walls and a roof, it was a tall, dim corridor lit only from the ends.

Silence, then Tutankhamun

Under Akhenaten work stopped and Amun's name was hacked out. The young Tutankhamun, restoring the old gods around 1330 BC, had the walls carved with the Opet festival. Horemheb later put his own name over Tutankhamun's, and Seti I added more.

The way there: west wall

Walking in from Ramesses II's court, the west wall is on your right. It shows the journey from Karnak: priests carry the gods' boats out of the temple, the boats are towed upriver by barges and men on the bank, with soldiers, chariots and crowds alongside.

The party

Look for the details of the festival: Nubian dancers and drummers, acrobats, musicians with harps and clappers, priests with incense, and butchers slaughtering bulls for the feast that fed the crowds.

The way back: east wall

On the opposite wall the procession returns to Karnak after the rites in the sanctuary, the boats going downriver with the current. Many scenes are damaged, but the rows of figures and boats are easy to follow.

The Opet festival

The reason the temple exists: once a year, for up to four weeks during the flood, the gods of Karnak came to stay.

Amun's southern home

Luxor Temple was Ipet-resyt, 'the southern sanctuary' of Amun. It was not a temple for daily worship of a king, but a place where the god, and the king's divine power, were renewed each year.

The procession

At the flood, the statues of Amun, Mut and Khonsu left Karnak in their gilded boats, carried by priests along the avenue of sphinxes or towed upriver, with music, dancers, acrobats and food for the crowds.

The king's ka

In the dark of the sanctuary the king met the god and was united with his royal ka, his divine double. He came out reborn as the son of Amun, fit to rule another year.

Read it on the walls

The colonnade walls show the whole festival as a strip cartoon: the boats leaving Karnak on the west side, the return on the east, with soldiers, musicians and butchers. They were carved for Tutankhamun around 1330 BC.

Layers of history

Few places in Egypt show so many ages one on top of the other.

Amenhotep III

Built the core of the temple around 1380 BC: the sanctuary, the birth room, the hypostyle, the sun court and the colonnade.

Ramesses II

A century later added the court, the First Pylon, the obelisks and the colossi, about 1270 BC.

Alexander the Great

Rebuilt the barque shrine in his own name around 330 BC, to show he was the true heir of the pharaohs.

The Romans

Turned the temple into the heart of a military camp around AD 300 and painted the emperors' chapel.

Christians

Built churches in and around the temple in the 5th and 6th centuries; traces remain near the entrance.

Muslims

Raised the mosque of Abu el-Haggag on top of the buried court in the 13th century. Four religions have prayed on this spot.

Luxor Temple through time

  1. c. 1470 BC

    Hatshepsut and Thutmose III build a way station for Amun's boat here, later kept in Ramesses II's court.

  2. c. 1390–1353 BC

    Amenhotep III builds the temple, from the sanctuary to the colonnade.

  3. c. 1330 BC

    Under Tutankhamun the colonnade walls are carved with the Opet festival.

  4. c. 1270 BC

    Ramesses II adds the court, the First Pylon, two obelisks and colossal statues.

  5. c. 370 BC

    Nectanebo I lines the avenue to Karnak with sphinxes.

  6. c. 330 BC

    The barque shrine is rebuilt in the name of Alexander the Great.

  7. c. AD 300

    A Roman legion's camp surrounds the temple; the emperors' chapel is painted.

  8. 13th century

    The mosque of Abu el-Haggag is built on the buried court.

  9. 1836

    One of the two obelisks is set up in the Place de la Concorde, Paris.

  10. 1880s

    Gaston Maspero begins clearing the temple of the village built on top of it.

  11. 1989

    The cachette of 26 statues is found in the sun court.

  12. 2021

    The avenue of sphinxes to Karnak is reopened.
