The Temple of Seti I at Abydos

Abydos was the holy city of Osiris, god of the dead, where every Egyptian hoped to make a pilgrimage. Here Seti I built a temple unlike any other: L-shaped, with seven chapels side by side and some of the most beautiful reliefs ever carved, still bright with colour. In a side gallery he and the young Ramesses II honour 76 kings before them, the famous Abydos King List.

Through Seti's temple

Twelve stops, from the courts to the seven chapels, then the side wing, the Osireion and Ramesses II's temple. Tap a number on the plan.

Simplified plan, not to scale; positions are approximate.

  1. 1

    The courts and the pylon

    The temple was entered through a pylon and two open courts, now mostly ruined. Seti I died before the front was finished, and his son Ramesses II completed it, so the outer parts carry Ramesses's quicker sunk relief.

    Ramesses II ↗

  2. 2

    The portico

    A row of twelve square pillars carved with Ramesses II before the gods. There were once seven doors here, one for each of the seven chapels inside; Ramesses walled up all but the central one.

  3. 3

    The first hypostyle hall

    Twenty-four papyrus columns, decorated after Seti's death by Ramesses II. Compare the deep, simpler carving here with the next hall: the change of king shows in the stone.

  4. 4

    The second hypostyle hall

    Thirty-six columns and some of the finest raised relief ever carved in Egypt, made for Seti I himself: delicate faces, fine wigs and jewellery, the king offering to the gods in scenes of perfect calm. Much original colour survives.

    Seti I ↗

  5. 5

    The seven chapels

    A row of seven sanctuaries, unique in Egypt, each with its own door and axis: for the deified Seti, Ptah, Ra-Horakhty, Amun-Ra, Osiris, Isis and Horus. Their vaulted ceilings, cut from great blocks, keep their painted stars, and the walls show the daily ritual of washing, dressing and feeding the god.

    Ptah ↗ · Amun ↗ · Ra ↗

  6. 6

    The Osiris suite

    Behind the chapel of Osiris lies a hall and further chapels for Osiris, Isis and Horus: the mysteries of the god's death and rebirth, at the heart of Abydos. Among the reliefs is Isis as a kite hovering over Osiris, conceiving their son Horus.

    Osiris ↗ · Isis ↗ · Horus ↗

  7. 7

    The Gallery of the Lists

    In the side wing, Seti I and the young prince Ramesses offer to 76 cartouches of earlier kings, from Menes, the first, down to Seti: the Abydos King List, one of the most important documents of Egyptian history.

    The King List ↓

  8. 8

    The hall of Sokar and Nefertem

    A columned hall for Sokar, the falcon god of the Memphite necropolis, and Nefertem, the young god of the lotus, with small chapels beyond: Memphis honoured inside the temple of Osiris.

  9. 9

    The corridor of the bull

    A staircase corridor leading out towards the back of the temple. On its wall Ramesses II, still a prince, lassoes a wild bull while his father Seti holds it by the tail, a lively scene from a ritual for the gods.

  10. 10

    The 'helicopter' hieroglyphs

    On a beam high in the first hypostyle hall, some signs look like a helicopter and a submarine. They are an accident: Seti's titles were filled with plaster and recut with Ramesses's, and as the plaster fell out the two sets of signs mixed. A famous internet myth, and a nice lesson in re-carving.

  11. 11

    The Osireion

    Behind the temple, sunk deep in the ground, lies a strange building of huge granite pillars around a water channel: a symbolic tomb of Osiris made for Seti I, perhaps imagined as the island that rose from the first waters. Groundwater often floods it; you look down on it from above.

    Osiris ↗

  12. 12

    The temple of Ramesses II

    A short walk to the north-west, Ramesses II's own smaller temple keeps some of the brightest colours in Egypt and scenes of the battle of Kadesh. A second King List from it is in the British Museum.

    Ramesses II ↗

The Abydos King List

A roll-call of the ancestors, carved so that Seti could offer to every legitimate king before him.

Schematic layout of the list, not to scale.

76 kings in order

Two rows of 38 cartouches run from Menes, the legendary unifier of Egypt, to Seti I himself, with a third row repeating Seti's names. Seti and his son burn incense and recite praise to them as ancestors.

Who is missing

It is an official family tree, not a history book. Hatshepsut is left out, and so are Akhenaten, Smenkhkare, Tutankhamun and Ay, the kings of the Amarna age; the foreign Hyksos and most rulers of the troubled intermediate periods are skipped too.

Why it matters

With the Turin papyrus and the Palermo Stone, it is one of the main sources for the order of the kings. It was cleared and copied in 1864; a damaged twin from Ramesses II's temple has been in the British Museum since 1837.

Prince Ramesses

The boy reading from a papyrus roll beside Seti is the future Ramesses II, shown with the sidelock of youth. Seti was preparing his son, and the dynasty's claim, for the future.

The art of Seti I

Many think these are the finest reliefs of the New Kingdom.

Raised and sunk relief

Seti's craftsmen cut away the background, leaving figures standing out in fine raised relief; Ramesses's team cut figures into the surface, sunk relief, quicker and bolder. The two halls side by side are the best place in Egypt to see the difference.

Colour

Away from the sun, the chapels and inner halls keep their blues, reds and yellows, often brighter than anything at Karnak or Luxor.

Seti's face

Seti I is shown again and again with a gentle, idealised face. His mummy, found in the royal cache in 1881, is one of the best preserved of any pharaoh, and it looks strikingly like his portraits.

Built in white limestone

Unlike the sandstone temples further south, the inner temple is of fine white limestone, which allowed the extraordinary detail of the carving.

Abydos, city of Osiris

The tomb of Osiris

Abydos was the holiest place in Egypt for Osiris, god of the dead: here his head, or all of his body, was said to be buried after Seth killed him. Every Egyptian hoped to make the pilgrimage, in life or in death.

The first kings

Behind the town, at Umm el-Qa'ab, the kings of the 1st Dynasty and their predecessors were buried around 3000 BC. Later Egyptians believed one of these tombs, King Djer's, was the tomb of Osiris himself.

Mysteries and stelae

Each year a festival re-enacted the death and rebirth of Osiris, with a procession to his tomb. Thousands of people set up stelae and small chapels along the route so as to share in it for ever.

Omm Sety

An Englishwoman, Dorothy Eady, believed she had been a priestess in Seti's temple in a former life. She lived at Abydos from 1956 until her death in 1981, known as Omm Sety, and helped the archaeologists working here.

Abydos through time

  1. c. 3200–2900 BC

    The earliest kings are buried at Umm el-Qa'ab, behind Abydos.

  2. c. 2000–1700 BC

    Abydos becomes the great centre of Osiris; pilgrims leave thousands of stelae.

  3. c. 1290 BC

    Seti I begins his temple and the Osireion.

  4. c. 1279 BC

    Seti dies; Ramesses II finishes the front of the temple and builds his own nearby.

  5. c. 1213–1203 BC

    Merneptah decorates parts of the Osireion.

  6. Greek and Roman times

    Visitors call the temple the Memnonium and leave graffiti; the oracle of the god Bes is consulted here.

  7. 1837

    The King List from Ramesses II's temple is taken to London.

  8. 1864

    Seti's King List is cleared and copied.

  9. 1903

    The Osireion is discovered by Margaret Murray working with Flinders Petrie.

  10. 1956–1981

    Omm Sety lives and works at Abydos.

For our visit

Inside first

The best things are indoors: the second hypostyle hall, the chapels and the King List. Give them most of your time.

Compare the halls

Walk from the first hypostyle into the second and look closely at the carving: Ramesses's sunk relief, then Seti's raised relief.

Find the helicopter

Ask the guide or a guardian to point out the beam in the first hall; it is high up and easy to miss.

Same day as Dendera

Abydos and Dendera make a long day from Luxor; Abydos is the farther one, so it is usually seen first.
