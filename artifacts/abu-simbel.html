Abu Simbel, two temples

Ramesses II had two temples cut straight into the sandstone cliffs above the Nile, near Egypt's southern frontier: a great one for the state gods and for himself, and a smaller one for the goddess Hathor and his queen Nefertari. Both face east, so the rising sun lights their fronts. In the 1960s both were cut into blocks and rebuilt higher up, out of reach of Lake Nasser.

The Great Temple

For the gods, and for Ramesses as a god

Gods
Ra-Horakhty, Amun-Ra and Ptah, with Ramesses himself, deified
Front
About 30 m high and 35 m wide; four seated colossi of the king, each about 20 m tall
Inside
A hall of eight Osiris pillars, a second hall, a vestibule and the sanctuary, over 60 m into the rock
Famous for
The sun reaching the sanctuary twice a year, and the reliefs of the battle of Kadesh
The Small Temple

For Hathor and Queen Nefertari

Goddess
Hathor of Ibshek, and Nefertari, the king's great royal wife
Front
Six standing statues about 10 m tall: four of Ramesses and two of Nefertari, at the same size as the king
Inside
A hall of six Hathor pillars, a vestibule with two side rooms, and the sanctuary
Famous for
One of only a few Egyptian temples dedicated to a queen

The site today

The two temples stand about 100 m apart on the west shore of Lake Nasser, the Small Temple to the north. Since the rescue they sit inside two artificial hills, built over hollow concrete domes, about 65 m higher than and 200 m back from where they were carved.

The Great Temple, step by step

Walk in a straight line from the light into the dark: each room is smaller and lower than the last, as in every Egyptian temple, until you reach the gods in the sanctuary. Tap a number on the plan.

Simplified plan with approximate proportions. Entrance at the bottom (east).

  1. 1

    Four colossi of Ramesses

    The king sits four times across the front, each figure about 20 m high, wearing the double crown. Small figures of his mother Tuya, Queen Nefertari and some of his children stand between and beside the legs.

  2. 2

    The broken colossus

    The second figure from the left lost its head and chest in an earthquake soon after the temple was finished; the pieces still lie at its feet, and the rescuers left them there. On a leg of this figure, Greek mercenaries of Psamtik II scratched their names in about 592 BC, some of the oldest Greek writing known.

    Psamtik II card ↗

  3. 3

    Ra-Horakhty above the door

    In a niche over the entrance stands the falcon-headed sun god. At his sides are a sceptre (user) and the goddess Ma'at: read together with the god (Ra) they spell User-Maat-Ra, the king's throne name. The whole front is a giant name tag.

    Ra on the gods page ↗

  4. 4

    The baboons at the top

    Along the cornice sits a row of baboons with raised arms. Baboons chatter at dawn, so the Egyptians saw them as greeting the rising sun. Look up before you go in.

  5. 5

    Hall of the Osiris pillars

    Eight pillars, each fronted by a standing Ramesses wrapped like Osiris, the god of rebirth, line the first hall. The ceiling is painted with flying vultures, the goddess Nekhbet protecting the king.

  6. 6

    The battle of Kadesh

    The whole north wall (on your right as you walk in) shows Ramesses' battle against the Hittites in Syria, c. 1274 BC: the army on the march, the camp, spies being beaten, and the king charging alone in his chariot. Egypt claimed a victory; in fact it was a draw, followed years later by a peace treaty.

  7. 7

    Side rooms

    Small rooms open off the first hall, probably stores for temple equipment and offerings. Their walls show the king offering to the gods.

  8. 8

    The second hall

    Four square pillars and scenes of the king and Nefertari before the gods. The rooms get smaller and darker from here on.

  9. 9

    The vestibule

    A narrow cross-room before the sanctuary, with three doors: the middle one leads to the gods, the two side ones to small chapels.

  10. 10

    The sanctuary and the sun

    Four seated statues sit against the back wall: Ptah, Amun-Ra, the deified Ramesses and Ra-Horakhty. Around 22 February and 22 October the rising sun shines straight down the temple and lights three of them. Ptah, linked with the underworld, stays in the dark. The dates moved by about a day when the temple was rebuilt.

The Small Temple, step by step

About 100 m to the north. Smaller and simpler, with some of the most delicate carving of Ramesses' reign.

Simplified plan with approximate proportions. Entrance at the bottom (east).

  1. 1

    Six standing statues

    On each side of the door stand Ramesses, Nefertari and Ramesses again, each about 10 m tall, stepping forward out of the rock between sloping buttresses. Their children stand small beside their legs.

  2. 2

    A queen as tall as the king

    Queens were usually shown knee-high beside their husbands. Here Nefertari is the same size as Ramesses, a sign of how highly he placed her. The inscriptions say the temple was made for her, "for whose sake the very sun does shine".

  3. 3

    Hall of Hathor pillars

    Six pillars topped with the face of Hathor, with cow's ears, hold up the hall. The walls show Ramesses smiting enemies and Nefertari offering to the gods, carved with unusual grace.

    Hathor ↗

  4. 4

    Nefertari crowned by the goddesses

    In the vestibule, Hathor and Isis place the crown on Nefertari's head: the queen is received among the gods. Two small side rooms open off this cross-room.

    Isis ↗

  5. 5

    Hathor as a cow

    In the sanctuary, Hathor in the form of a cow steps out of the rock, protecting the king who stands below her head.

Lost, found, and moved

For over two thousand years sand slowly buried the temples. Then the new Aswan High Dam threatened to drown them for ever.

  1. c. 1264 BC

    Work begins under Ramesses II. The temples are finished some twenty years later, around year 24 of his reign.

  2. c. 592 BC

    Greek, Carian and Phoenician soldiers of Psamtik II, back from a campaign in Nubia, carve graffiti on the colossi.

  3. AD 1813

    The Swiss traveller Johann Ludwig Burckhardt sees the heads of the colossi sticking out of the sand.

  4. 1817

    Giovanni Belzoni digs his way to the door of the Great Temple and goes inside.

  5. 1964–1968

    An international team led by UNESCO cuts both temples, cliff and all, into more than 1,000 blocks of up to about 30 tonnes, lifts them 65 m and rebuilds them about 200 m back from the river, inside artificial hills on concrete domes. The rebuilt temples open in September 1968.

  6. 1979

    Abu Simbel becomes part of the UNESCO World Heritage site "Nubian Monuments from Abu Simbel to Philae". Philae, our Day 3, was rescued the same way.

For our visit

Morning light

The fronts face east, so they are lit by the morning sun and fall into shade in the afternoon. Take the facade photos early.

Great Temple first

Go into the Great Temple, then walk the 100 m along the shore to the Small Temple.

Look right for Kadesh

In the first hall, the battle reliefs cover the right-hand (north) wall from floor to ceiling.

Find the seams

Look closely at the facades and ceilings: the cuts where the blocks were rejoined in the 1960s are still visible.
