Deir el-Bahari · west bank of Luxor · c. 1473–1458 BC · Day 6 of the Alimaña tour

The Temple of Hatshepsut

Hatshepsut, a woman who ruled Egypt as king, built her temple for the god Amun and for her own memory in a bay of sheer cliffs on the west bank, right next to a temple 500 years older. Three terraces of colonnades climb towards the rock, linked by long ramps; its ancient name was Djeser-djeseru, 'Holy of Holies'. Its walls tell of a trading voyage to the land of Punt and of the queen's divine birth, and show where her successor tried to erase her.

The temple

Djeser-djeseru

Where
Deir el-Bahari, a bay in the cliffs of western Thebes, facing Karnak across the Nile
When
Built c. 1473–1458 BC, 18th Dynasty
For
Amun above all, with chapels for Hathor, Anubis, Ra-Horakhty and the cult of the queen and her father
Not a tomb
A temple for her cult; her burial was in the Valley of the Kings, just behind the cliff
The builder

Hatshepsut

Reign
c. 1479–1458 BC, first as regent, then as king with her stepson Thutmose III
Names
Hatshepsut, 'Foremost of noble women'; as king, Maatkare
Architect
Probably her steward Senenmut
Model
The terraced temple of Mentuhotep II next door, which she copied and outdid

Up the three terraces

Twelve stops from the approach to the sanctuary in the rock. The temple faces east, so as you walk up, south is on your left. Tap a number on the drawing.

Simplified front view, not to scale. The courts behind each colonnade are hidden in this view.

  1. 1

    The approach

    A causeway lined with sphinxes of the queen once ran here from a valley temple on the edge of the fields, about a kilometre away; it was the road of Amun's boat at the yearly festival. Today a little electric train brings visitors from the car park to the foot of the temple.

  2. 2

    The lower court

    A garden in front of the temple: pits cut in the rock held trees, among them perhaps the incense trees brought from Punt, and two T-shaped pools stood beside the ramp. Almost nothing grows here now, so imagine shade and water.

  3. 3

    The lower portico

    Two colonnades on either side of the ramp. On the left (south) a barge carries two giant obelisks from Aswan to Karnak, the queen's pair there; on the right the queen appears as a sphinx crushing Egypt's enemies, and in the marshes.

    Karnak ↗

  4. 4

    The ramps

    A wide ramp climbs to the middle terrace and a second one to the top, on the temple's single axis, which points across the Nile towards Karnak, about 4 km away. Look for the lions carved on the parapets.

  5. 5

    The Punt colonnade

    The most famous reliefs: the expedition the queen sent in Year 9 to Punt, somewhere on the coast of the Red Sea or the Horn of Africa. Ships unload at houses on stilts; the chief Parahu and his large wife Ati greet the Egyptians, who return with gold, ebony, panther skins, baboons and 31 myrrh trees in baskets. The block with Ati here is a cast; the original is in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.

  6. 6

    The birth colonnade

    Hatshepsut's claim in pictures. Amun visits queen Ahmose in the form of her husband Thutmose I; the ram-headed potter god Khnum shapes the child and her ka on his wheel, and the frog goddess Heqet gives them life. The child born is drawn as a boy: the scenes follow the old story of a king's divine birth.

    Amun ↗ · Khnum ↗

  7. 7

    The chapel of Hathor

    At the south end of the middle terrace, with columns topped by the cow-eared face of Hathor, lady of this western mountain. The queen is shown drinking milk from the Hathor cow. Behind the doors of the inner shrines small kneeling figures of Senenmut, her steward and architect, were carved where few would see them.

    Hathor ↗

  8. 8

    The chapel of Anubis

    At the north end, with sixteen-sided columns and the best colours in the temple. Many of Hatshepsut's figures were chiselled out; those of Thutmose III, her stepson, were left untouched beside them.

    Anubis ↗

  9. 9

    The upper portico

    A row of tall statues of Hatshepsut as Osiris, crossed arms holding crook and flail, with the double crown. They were smashed after her reign; the ones you see were put together from fragments by the Polish–Egyptian mission working here since 1961.

    Osiris ↗

  10. 10

    The festival court

    An open court with a double colonnade. Once a year, at the Beautiful Festival of the Valley, Amun's barque crossed the river from Karnak and spent the night here, while Thebans visited the tombs of their families and feasted with the dead.

  11. 11

    The sanctuary of Amun

    Cut straight into the cliff on the temple's axis. Under Ptolemy VIII, some 1,300 years later, an extra room was added for two deified wise men, Imhotep, builder of the Step Pyramid, and Amenhotep son of Hapu, and it became a place of healing.

    Ptolemy VIII ↗

  12. 12

    The sun court and the royal chapels

    To the right (north) an open court with a great altar for the sun god Ra-Horakhty; to the left (south) chapels for the cult of Hatshepsut and of her father Thutmose I. Hidden above, a temple of Thutmose III was crushed by a rockfall in antiquity.

    Thutmose I ↗ · Ra ↗

The woman who was king

Other women had held power in Egypt, but Hatshepsut is the one who ruled longest with the full titles of a king, and whose monuments say most about how she did it.

From regent to king

Daughter of Thutmose I and wife of her half-brother Thutmose II. When he died, around 1479 BC, she ruled for his small son Thutmose III; within a few years she had herself crowned king, and the two reigned together, with her firmly in charge, for about 20 years.

Dressed for the role

Early statues show a woman in a queen's dress. Later ones give her a king's kilt, headcloth and false beard, and a male body. It was not a disguise: the office of pharaoh had a fixed image, and she took it on. Her texts often keep the feminine grammar.

Senenmut

Her steward and tutor of her daughter Neferure, and probably the designer of this temple. He had himself carved in hidden corners here and cut a tomb whose corridors run under the temple forecourt, with the oldest astronomical ceiling known. He drops out of the record before her death.

A peaceful reign

Building at Karnak, the obelisks, trade with Punt and the Sinai mines rather than great wars, although some campaigns are recorded. Her name as king, Maatkare, means 'Truth is the soul of Ra'.

Erased

Some 20 years after her death, late in Thutmose III's reign, her images as king were hacked out and her statues thrown into a quarry nearby. It was probably not revenge but politics: to make the line of male kings look unbroken for his son Amenhotep II.

Found again

Her tomb in the Valley of the Kings (KV20) was empty. In 2007 a mummy from the small tomb KV60 was identified as hers, partly from a tooth found in a box bearing her name; not every scholar is convinced.

Deir el-Bahari through time

  1. c. 2000 BC

    Mentuhotep II, who reunited Egypt, builds his terraced temple and tomb in the bay of Deir el-Bahari.

  2. c. 1479 BC

    Thutmose II dies; Hatshepsut rules for the young Thutmose III.

  3. c. 1473 BC

    She is crowned king. Work on Djeser-djeseru, 'Holy of Holies', begins about now and goes on for some 15 years.

  4. c. 1471 BC

    Year 9: the expedition to Punt.

  5. c. 1458 BC

    Hatshepsut dies; Thutmose III rules alone.

  6. c. 1440 BC

    Her images and statues are destroyed; Thutmose III builds his own temple above hers.

  7. c. 140 BC

    Ptolemy VIII adds a chapel for Imhotep and Amenhotep son of Hapu in the sanctuary.

  8. 7th century AD

    A Coptic monastery settles on the upper terrace, which gives the site its Arabic name, Deir el-Bahari, 'the northern monastery'.

  9. 1881

    In a shaft in the cliffs just south of the temple (DB320) the Royal Cache is found, with the mummies of Seti I, Ramesses II and many other kings.

  10. 1890s

    Édouard Naville clears the temple for the Egypt Exploration Fund.

  11. 1920s

    Herbert Winlock recovers the smashed statues of the queen from the quarry; many are in New York and Cairo.

  12. 1961

    The Polish–Egyptian mission begins its long work of study and restoration.

  13. 2007

    A mummy from KV60 is identified as Hatshepsut.

For our visit

Light and heat

The bay faces east and holds the heat; the morning sun lights the colonnades. Sunglasses and water: the white stone glares.

Look for the gaps

Scan the walls for neat, chiselled-out outlines: that is where the queen stood. Thutmose III beside her is untouched.

Don't miss Punt

Middle terrace, left of the ramp: fish in the Red Sea, the houses on stilts and the trees carried in baskets.

Same morning

The Valley of the Kings lies just over the cliff behind the temple; an ancient path climbs over the ridge to it.
