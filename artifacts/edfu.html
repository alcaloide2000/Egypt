Edfu, the temple of Horus

Halfway between Luxor and Aswan, on the west bank, stands the most complete temple in Egypt: roof, walls, pylon and enclosure are all still there. The Ptolemies built it for Horus of Behdet, the falcon god, over 180 years, and carved its walls with the story of his fight against Seth and the rites performed for him every day. Buried under sand and houses for centuries, it came out almost intact.

Walking the temple, from the gate to the god

The visit starts at the birth house in front of the pylon and walks north through the court and the two halls, each darker and higher-floored than the last, to the sanctuary; then round the passage outside the temple and out past the nilometer. Tap a number on the plan.

  1. 1

    The birth house

    In front of the pylon, to the west, stands the mammisi built by Ptolemy VIII. Here the priests celebrated the birth of Harsomtus, the child of Horus and Hathor. Its reliefs show Hathor nursing the baby, and the dwarf god Bes, protector of births, grimaces from the tops of the columns.

    Bes ↗ · Hathor ↗

  2. 2

    The pylon

    About 36 m high and 79 m wide, one of the tallest temple gateways in Egypt, second only to the unfinished first pylon of Karnak. On both towers Ptolemy XII, Cleopatra's father, smites his enemies before Horus and Hathor. The slots in its face held flagpoles with pennants.

    Ptolemy XII ↗ · More about the pylon ↓

  3. 3

    The granite falcons

    Horus guards the doors as a falcon wearing the double crown. One granite falcon stands by the pylon gateway; the best-preserved stands in the court in front of the hypostyle hall, the most photographed statue in the temple.

    Horus ↗

  4. 4

    The court of offerings

    A great open court with 32 columns on three sides, each capital a different plant. Here the public could come at festivals; beyond the next doorway only priests went on.

    The columns ↓

  5. 5

    The outer hypostyle hall

    Eighteen columns in three rows hold up the roof. By the entrance are two tiny rooms: a library, whose wall lists the books it kept, and a room where the king, or the priest standing in for him, was purified before going in.

    The columns ↓

  6. 6

    The inner hypostyle hall

    Twelve columns, darker and lower: the Hall of Appearances, where the god's statue was brought out for processions. A small side room, the 'laboratory', has recipes for the incense and perfumed oils used in the rites carved on its walls.

  7. 7

    The offering hall and the stairs

    Food, drink and flowers were laid out here for Horus every day. Two staircases, one straight and one winding, lead to the roof; their walls show the priests carrying the god's statue up at New Year so that it could meet the sun.

  8. 8

    The New Year chapel

    A small chapel with its own open court beside the offering hall. On New Year's Day the statue rested here before going up to the roof; the ceiling shows the sky goddess Nut.

    Nut ↗

  9. 9

    The sanctuary

    The heart of the temple. The polished grey granite shrine at the back, made for Nectanebo II around 350 BC, is the oldest thing in the temple: the Ptolemies built their temple around it. In front stands a modern copy of the sacred boat that carried Horus's statue in processions.

    Nectanebo II ↗

  10. 10

    The chapels round the sanctuary

    A ring of small rooms opens off a corridor around the sanctuary, each for another god or a sacred object: Osiris, Min, Khonsu, Hathor, and the throne of Ra among them.

  11. 11

    Horus against Seth

    Between the temple and the enclosure wall runs a long passage. On the inner face of the west wall, the Triumph of Horus is told like a play: Horus, standing in a boat, harpoons his uncle Seth, shown as a small hippopotamus, to avenge his father Osiris.

    Seth ↗ · Osiris ↗

  12. 12

    The nilometer

    Outside the east side of the enclosure, a stairway runs down to the water table. Marks on its walls showed how high the flood would rise, and so how big the harvest, and the taxes, would be.

    Hapi ↗

The pylon

The great gateway is the first thing you see and the last part of the temple to be finished. It is a symbol, a billboard and a building in its own right.

The horizon in stone

The two towers copy the hieroglyph for horizon: two mountains with the sun rising between them. Walking through the gate meant crossing from the everyday world into the god's. The towers were also linked to Isis and Nephthys, guarding the way in.

Built last

About 36 m high and 79 m wide, roughly a twelve-storey building. The building texts on the walls say that the court, the pylon and the great doors were finished in 57 BC under Ptolemy XII, 180 years after the foundation.

The oldest royal image

The giant king grabbing his kneeling enemies by the hair and raising a mace is the same pose Narmer takes on the Narmer Palette, some 3,000 years earlier. Ptolemy XII, 'the Flute-player', was in fact a weak king who depended on Rome; on his pylon he is an all-conquering pharaoh.

Flags, stairs and windows

A pole with a pennant is also the hieroglyph for 'god', so the flags announced from far away that a god lived here. Each tower is hollow, with a staircase rising past small storerooms to the top, where priests fixed the flags and watched the sky; it is closed to visitors.

The columns and the art

Edfu's 62 columns, 32 in the court and 30 in the two halls, are where the Ptolemaic style shows best. Egyptian columns had always copied plants; by Ptolemaic times the sculptors combined many plants in one capital and covered every surface with carving.

Three ways to carve a plant in stone, simplified. Edfu's capitals are the third kind.

A garden in stone

A temple was a model of the first land that rose from the waters. Its columns are the plants of that first marsh, papyrus, lotus and palm, growing from the floor towards a ceiling painted as the sky, with vultures and stars.

Composite capitals

Each capital stacks two to four tiers of leaves, buds and open flowers over a bell, sometimes with palm fronds or bunches of grapes. Walk along the court: neighbouring capitals are almost never the same, though some match their partner across the axis.

Shafts covered in scenes

The shafts are not plain. Each carries small scenes of the king offering to the gods, with columns of hieroglyphs; at the foot, carved leaves wrap the stem as on a real plant, above a round base.

The screen walls

In front of the outer hall, the columns are joined by walls rising to about half their height, carved with the king being purified and crowned. It is a Ptolemaic idea: light enters from above, while the hall behind stays half hidden from the court.

Ptolemaic figures

The gods and kings are rounder and fleshier than in older temples: full faces, soft bellies, heavy limbs, carved deep. Outside, the carving is sunk into the stone to catch the hard sunlight; inside it is raised. Many faces and hands within reach were chiselled away after the temple closed.

Colour and smoke

Everything was once painted in strong blues, greens, reds and yellows; traces survive high on the capitals. The ceilings of the halls are black with soot from the fires of the people who later lived inside.

The Festival of the Beautiful Meeting

Once a year, in the third month of summer, the statue of Hathor left her temple at Dendera by boat and sailed upstream to visit her husband Horus at Edfu, stopping at temples on the way. Horus came out to meet her, and the two gods spent about two weeks together among feasts, offerings and visits to the tombs of the gods buried near Edfu, before Hathor sailed home. You will see Dendera, the other end of the journey, on Day 7.

Edfu through time

  1. Old Kingdom

    Edfu, ancient Behdet, is already a town and the capital of its province; tombs of its governors lie behind the temple.

  2. c. 350 BC

    Nectanebo II has the granite shrine made for an earlier temple on the site.

  3. 23 Aug 237 BC

    Ptolemy III lays the foundation of the present temple.

  4. 212 BC

    The main building is finished under Ptolemy IV.

  5. 142 BC

    After the decoration is done, the temple is dedicated under Ptolemy VIII.

  6. 140–124 BC

    The outer hypostyle hall is added in front.

  7. 57 BC

    Under Ptolemy XII the court, the pylon and the great doors are finished, 180 years after the foundation. Building texts on the walls record each stage.

  8. AD 4th–5th c.

    Once the temples are closed, Christians deface many faces; sand and later a village gradually fill and cover the temple.

  9. 1798–99

    Napoleon's scholars draw the temple half-buried, with houses on its roof.

  10. from 1860

    Auguste Mariette clears the sand and houses away, revealing the best-preserved temple in Egypt.

For our visit

By calèche

From the river the usual way in is a horse-drawn calèche through the town, a short, bumpy and memorable ride.

Find the falcon

The granite falcon in front of the hypostyle hall is the classic photo; go early before the groups gather round it.

Walk the passage

Many visitors skip it: go round between the temple and the enclosure wall for Horus harpooning Seth.

Watch the light

Inside, it gets darker with every hall towards the sanctuary, just as the builders meant.
