Karnak

The largest temple complex of the ancient world, home of Amun-Ra, king of the gods. For two thousand years pharaohs kept adding gateways, halls, obelisks and chapels, each in front of the last, so walking in from the river you go back in time. The heart of it is the walled precinct of Amun, about 25 hectares, with the Great Hypostyle Hall and its forest of 134 columns.

From the river to the god

Twelve stops along the main east–west axis, from the quay to the festival hall, then the lake and the open-air museum. Tap a number on the plan.

Simplified plan of the precinct of Amun, not to scale; positions are approximate.

  1. 1

    The quay and the ram avenue

    Karnak faced the river: boats moored at a quay in front of the temple, now high and dry. An avenue of ram-headed sphinxes leads to the gate; the ram was the sacred animal of Amun, and each one shelters a small statue of the king between its paws.

    Amun ↗

  2. 2

    The First Pylon

    The last and largest gateway, about 113 m wide, probably begun under Nectanebo I in the 4th century BC and never finished: behind it, on the inside, are remains of the mud-brick ramp used to raise the blocks.

    Nectanebo I ↗

  3. 3

    The Great Court

    On the left, the triple shrine of Seti II for the boats of Amun, Mut and Khonsu; in the middle, the one column still standing of the kiosk of the Kushite king Taharqa; on the right, the small temple of Ramesses III. In the far right corner, the Bubastite Portal lists the towns taken in Palestine by Sheshonq I, the 'Shishak' of the Bible, around 925 BC.

    Seti II ↗ · Taharqa ↗ · Ramesses III ↗

  4. 4

    The Second Pylon

    Built under Horemheb and filled with thousands of small blocks from Akhenaten's demolished temples to the Aten. In front stands a granite colossus of Ramesses II with a small princess at his feet, later re-inscribed by the high priest Pinedjem.

    Horemheb ↗ · Akhenaten ↗

  5. 5

    The Great Hypostyle Hall

    A forest of 134 columns over about 5,000 m², started under Seti I and finished by Ramesses II. The 12 central ones, about 21 m high, carried a raised roof with stone windows; the rest are shorter. Seti's reliefs are carved in fine raised relief, Ramesses's mostly in quicker sunk relief. Outside, the north wall shows Seti I's wars.

    Seti I ↗ · Ramesses II ↗ · The hall in section ↓

  6. 6

    The obelisk of Thutmose I

    Past Amenhotep III's Third Pylon, a narrow court holds the obelisk raised by Thutmose I, about 20 m tall, the oldest standing at Karnak. Here, at the time, was the front of the temple.

    Thutmose I ↗ · Amenhotep III ↗

  7. 7

    The obelisk of Hatshepsut

    Between the Fourth and Fifth Pylons rises the tallest ancient obelisk still standing in Egypt, about 29.5 m of Aswan granite, once tipped with gold-silver. Thutmose III later hid its lower part behind walls, which protected its inscriptions. The top of its fallen twin lies by the Sacred Lake.

    Hatshepsut ↗ · Her temple ↗

  8. 8

    The granite shrine of Philip Arrhidaeus

    The heart of the temple, where Amun's boat rested. The present shrine of pink granite was rebuilt around 320 BC in the name of Philip Arrhidaeus, the half-brother of Alexander the Great, who ruled in name only; inside, the colours are still bright.

    Alexander ↗

  9. 9

    The Middle Kingdom court

    An open, empty space: here stood the first stone temple of Amun, built by Senusret I around 1950 BC. Everything else at Karnak grew outwards from this spot.

    Senusret I ↗

  10. 10

    The Akh-menu

    Thutmose III's festival hall, 'Most splendid of monuments', with unusual columns shaped like tent poles, as in a royal campaign tent. In a side room, the 'Botanical Garden' shows plants and animals the king brought back from Syria; another room held the Karnak King List, now in the Louvre.

    Thutmose III ↗

  11. 11

    The Sacred Lake

    Where the priests purified themselves and the sacred geese of Amun swam. At its north-west corner a giant granite scarab of Amenhotep III stands on a pillar: tradition says walking round it seven times brings luck, or a wedding.

    Amenhotep III ↗

  12. 12

    The open-air museum

    North of the Great Court (extra ticket). Two chapels rebuilt from blocks found inside later pylons: the White Chapel of Senusret I, the oldest building at Karnak, with fine reliefs and a list of Egypt's provinces, and the Red Chapel of Hatshepsut, in red quartzite and black granite.

    Senusret I ↗ · Hatshepsut ↗

The Great Hypostyle Hall

About 102 by 53 m, big enough to hold Notre-Dame de Paris. The roof is gone in most places, but the columns still carry it in the centre, and you can see how the light came in.

Simplified cross-section across the hall, not to scale.

A papyrus marsh in stone

Every temple was a model of the world at creation. The columns are papyrus plants rising from the first marsh: open flowers in the sunlit centre, closed buds in the dark sides.

Light from above

The only light came through the stone grilles high above the central aisle. Walking in, priests went from bright sun into deep shade, towards the hidden god.

Two styles, two kings

Seti I decorated the north half in delicate raised relief; Ramesses II finished the south half mostly in deep sunk relief, quicker to carve and sharper in strong sun.

Fallen and raised

In October 1899, eleven columns collapsed after their foundations were soaked by groundwater. They were rebuilt over the next years; the columns still stand on shallow footings.

How to read Karnak

Ipet-sut

Its ancient name, 'the most select of places'. It was Amun's house on earth, and the richest temple in Egypt: in the time of Ramesses III it owned land, herds, ships and tens of thousands of workers.

Two thousand years of building

Some thirty pharaohs added to it, from Senusret I around 1950 BC to the Ptolemies and Romans. Each new gateway went in front of the last, so the oldest parts are deep inside and the youngest at the entrance.

A family of gods

Amun-Ra, king of the gods, lived here with his wife Mut, whose precinct lies to the south, and their son Khonsu, the moon god, whose temple is in the south-west corner. The war god Montu has his own precinct to the north.

The Opet festival

Each year at the flood, the boats of the three gods were carried along the 2.7 km avenue of sphinxes, or sailed upriver, to Luxor Temple, where the king's divine power was renewed. The avenue was cleared and reopened in 2021.

Karnak through time

  1. c. 1950 BC

    Senusret I builds the first stone temple and the White Chapel.

  2. c. 1500 BC

    Thutmose I adds the Fourth and Fifth Pylons and his obelisks.

  3. c. 1470 BC

    Hatshepsut raises her obelisks and builds the Red Chapel.

  4. c. 1445 BC

    Thutmose III builds the Akh-menu, the Sacred Lake and records his wars in the Annals.

  5. c. 1380 BC

    Amenhotep III builds the Third Pylon.

  6. c. 1350 BC

    Akhenaten builds temples to the Aten east of Karnak; after his death they are pulled down and their blocks used as fill.

  7. c. 1290–1213 BC

    Seti I and Ramesses II build and decorate the Great Hypostyle Hall.

  8. c. 925 BC

    Sheshonq I carves his Palestinian campaign on the Bubastite Portal.

  9. c. 690–664 BC

    The Kushite king Taharqa builds his kiosk in the Great Court.

  10. c. 370s BC

    Work begins on the First Pylon, probably under Nectanebo I; it is never finished.

  11. c. 320 BC

    The granite shrine is rebuilt in the name of Philip Arrhidaeus.

  12. 1899

    Eleven columns of the Hypostyle Hall collapse; they are re-erected.

  13. 2021

    The avenue of sphinxes to Luxor Temple is reopened.

For our visit

Late afternoon

After the west bank, Karnak is best as the sun drops: the light slants through the hypostyle columns and the stone turns gold.

Look for the colours

High on the columns and under the architraves of the Hypostyle Hall, the original paint survives, recently cleaned.

Don't get lost

The site is huge. Follow the main axis to the Akh-menu, then turn south to the lake; the café is by the lake.

Sound and light

There is an evening sound and light show in the temple, a different way to see the hall lit up.
