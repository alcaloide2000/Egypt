The Valley of the Kings

For almost 500 years, the pharaohs of the New Kingdom were buried in a dry valley behind the cliffs of western Thebes, under a peak shaped like a pyramid. Instead of building pyramids, they cut their tombs deep into the rock and covered the walls with the books that guided the dead king through the night with the sun. More than 60 tombs are known, numbered KV1 to KV64 in the order they were found; only Tutankhamun's was found nearly intact.

How a royal tomb works

Each king began his tomb as soon as he came to the throne, and the workers dug until he died, so long reigns made long tombs. Early tombs turn a sharp corner; from the end of the 18th Dynasty they run straight into the hill, the way the sun travels through the night.

Simplified profile of a straight tomb of the 19th–20th Dynasties. Real tombs run from about 30 to 137 m into the rock.

The tombs to know

Twelve tombs, from the visitor centre into the valley. Which ones are open changes from day to day, so check the board at the entrance and choose three. Tap a number on the map.

  1. 1

    The visitor centre

    Start at the glass-covered model of the valley, which shows how the tombs run deep into the hills, then take the little electric train (the 'tuf-tuf') up to the tombs. The standard ticket opens three tombs of your choice from those open that day; a few cost extra.

  2. 2

    Tutankhamun (KV62)

    A small tomb for a king who died at about 19, around 1323 BC. Only the burial chamber is painted: his successor Ay performs the Opening of the Mouth on the dead king, and the baboons of the first hour of the night watch from the wall. His mummy lies in a glass case and his quartzite sarcophagus is still here; the treasures Howard Carter found on 4 November 1922 are in the Grand Egyptian Museum, our Day 9. Extra ticket.

    Tutankhamun ↗

  3. 3

    Ramesses V and VI (KV9)

    Begun for Ramesses V and finished for Ramesses VI. The burial chamber ceiling is the most spectacular in the valley: the sky goddess Nut stretched out twice, swallowing the sun in the evening and giving birth to it at dawn. The rubble and workmen's huts from digging this tomb covered the entrance of Tutankhamun's, just below, and hid it for 3,000 years. Extra ticket.

    Ramesses VI ↗ · Nut ↗

  4. 4

    Seti I (KV17)

    The longest tomb in the valley, about 137 m, and the most finely carved: every wall in painted raised relief, with the first astronomical ceiling over the burial chamber. Giovanni Belzoni found it in 1817; the alabaster sarcophagus is now in Sir John Soane's Museum in London. Often closed, and the most expensive ticket when open.

    Seti I ↗

  5. 5

    Ramesses III (KV11)

    Begun by his father Setnakht and finished for Ramesses III. Small side rooms off the first corridor show unusual scenes of daily life and the royal storerooms, among them two blind harpists singing to the gods, which gave it its old name, the Tomb of the Harpers.

    Ramesses III ↗

  6. 6

    Merneptah (KV8)

    The thirteenth son of Ramesses II, who outlived twelve older brothers. A long, steep corridor leads down to a hall where his huge pink granite sarcophagus lids still lie; on the inner lid the king is carved as Osiris.

    Merneptah ↗

  7. 7

    Ramesses IV (KV2)

    Near the entrance and open since antiquity: Greek and Roman tourists and later Coptic monks scratched their names on the walls. A plan of this very tomb drawn on papyrus survives in Turin, one of the oldest architectural plans in the world.

    Ramesses IV ↗

  8. 8

    Ramesses IX (KV6)

    A bright, easy tomb near the centre. Under this king the great tomb-robbery trials were held: papyri record the interrogation of the thieves who had plundered royal burials.

    Ramesses IX ↗

  9. 9

    Tausert and Setnakht (KV14)

    Begun for Tausert (Twosret), a queen who ruled as pharaoh in her own right; Setnakht, founder of the next dynasty, took it over and dug on, so it has two burial chambers, one behind the other.

    Twosret ↗ · Setnakhte ↗

  10. 10

    Thutmose III (KV34)

    Hidden high in a cleft at the far end of the valley and reached by steep metal stairs. The burial chamber has the rounded shape of a cartouche, and its walls show the Amduat drawn like an enormous unrolled papyrus, with stick-like figures on a yellowish ground; the hall before it lists 741 gods.

    Thutmose III ↗

  11. 11

    Amenhotep II (KV35)

    A deep tomb in the western branch. In 1898 Victor Loret found in it a hiding place with the mummies of nine other kings, among them Thutmose IV, Amenhotep III and Merneptah, moved there by priests around 1000 BC to save them from robbers. Often closed.

  12. 12

    The sons of Ramesses II (KV5)

    The largest tomb in Egypt, with more than 120 corridors and rooms, built for the many sons of Ramesses II. Long thought to be a small, flooded tomb, it was explored by Kent Weeks from 1995. Closed to visitors; Ramesses II's own tomb, KV7, lies nearby, badly damaged by floods.

    Ramesses II ↗

The Books of the Netherworld

The paintings are not decoration: they are guidebooks for the afterlife, written for the king alone. Recognising them makes every tomb easier to read.

The sun's night journey

Every evening the sun god Ra entered the underworld in his boat and sailed through it for the twelve hours of the night, fighting the serpent Apophis, to be reborn at dawn. The dead king joined him. The tomb walls are the map of that journey, hour by hour.

The Amduat

'What is in the Underworld', the oldest of these books, with the twelve hours of the night. In the early tombs, like Thutmose III's, it is drawn like a giant papyrus unrolled along the walls, with simple stick-like figures.

The Book of Gates

The night again, but each hour ends at a gate guarded by a serpent that only the right names will open. It appears from the tomb of Horemheb on, in full colour.

The Litany of Ra

A hymn listing the 75 forms of the sun god, painted near the entrances of the Ramesside tombs, often with the sun disk between a ram-headed god and a beetle.

Caverns and the Earth

Later books show the underworld as caverns and the depths of the earth, where the enemies of Ra are punished, while the sun disk and the body of Osiris are protected and renewed.

The sky on the ceiling

Burial chambers often have a ceiling painted as the sky. Seti I's shows the constellations; in Ramesses VI's the long body of Nut arches over the room twice, for the day and the night.

The valley through time

  1. c. 1500 BC

    Thutmose I is the first king known to be buried in the valley, hidden away from his temple by the river; tombs and temples are now kept apart.

  2. c. 1425 BC

    Thutmose III is buried in KV34, high in the cliff.

  3. c. 1323 BC

    Tutankhamun is buried in a small tomb, KV62, after a short reign.

  4. c. 1279 BC

    Seti I is buried in KV17, the longest and finest tomb.

  5. c. 1157 BC

    The tomb workers of Deir el-Medina down tools over late rations under Ramesses III: the first recorded strike in history.

  6. c. 1110 BC

    Under Ramesses IX, tomb robbers are put on trial; the papyri record their confessions.

  7. c. 1070 BC

    Ramesses XI's tomb, KV4, the last royal tomb, is left unfinished and never used.

  8. c. 1000 BC

    Priests unwrap, rewrap and hide the royal mummies in two caches, one in KV35.

  9. 1817

    Giovanni Belzoni finds the tomb of Seti I.

  10. 1881 and 1898

    The two royal caches are found: at Deir el-Bahari, then in KV35.

  11. 4 Nov 1922

    Howard Carter finds the steps to Tutankhamun's tomb, almost intact.

  12. 1995

    Kent Weeks reveals the vast extent of KV5, the tomb of Ramesses II's sons.

  13. 2005

    KV63, the first new find since 1922, turns out to hold coffins and embalming material, but no mummy.

For our visit

Choose your three

Good choices if open: Ramesses III (KV11), Merneptah (KV8) and Ramesses IV (KV2) or Thutmose III (KV34). Tutankhamun and Ramesses V/VI need extra tickets.

Go early

The valley is a sun trap with almost no shade, and the tombs get crowded and stuffy later in the morning.

Photos

Photography rules change; ask at the ticket office, and never use a flash, which fades the paint.

Read the ceilings

Look up as you go deeper: yellow stars on blue mean you are under the night sky, close to the king.
